## Rank choice is a bias-variance trade-off, not a "bigger is better" knob

The intuitive assumption — higher rank means strictly better fine-tuning — doesn't hold
in practice. Biderman et al. (2024) found LoRA consistently underperforms full
fine-tuning on tasks requiring substantial new knowledge (e.g. code or math domains far
from the pretraining distribution), but *matches or exceeds* full fine-tuning on
instruction-following and chat-style tasks, and critically, **forgets less** of the
base model's original capabilities. This makes rank selection a real trade-off: low
rank acts as a strong regularizer that constrains how much the model can drift from its
pretrained behavior, which is a feature when you want targeted adaptation without
catastrophic forgetting, and a limitation when the target task genuinely requires new
capability the base model lacks.

## Why `lora_alpha / r` scaling matters more than either number alone

The LoRA update is scaled by `alpha / r`, not by `alpha` or `r` independently. This
means `r=8, alpha=16` and `r=32, alpha=64` produce the *same effective scaling factor*
(2.0) but very different adapter capacities. Two implications follow:

1. **Rank sweeps require holding the scaling factor fixed** (typically by scaling
   `alpha` proportionally with `r`), otherwise you're confounding "more capacity" with
   "different effective learning rate" — a common source of misleading LoRA ablations
   in practice.
2. **rsLoRA** (rank-stabilized LoRA) changes the scaling to `alpha / sqrt(r)` instead of
   `alpha / r`, because empirically the `1/r` scaling causes gradient magnitudes to
   collapse at high rank, making large-rank LoRA training unstable without a learning
   rate increase. This is why PEFT exposes `use_rslora=True` as an option — it's not
   just an alternative parameterization, it changes what rank values are even usable.

## DoRA: decomposing magnitude from direction

**DoRA (Weight-Decomposed LoRA)** splits the frozen weight into a magnitude vector and
a direction matrix, applying LoRA only to the directional component while learning the
magnitude separately:

```python
from peft import LoraConfig
config = LoraConfig(r=16, lora_alpha=32, use_dora=True, target_modules=["q_proj", "v_proj"])
```

The motivation: standard LoRA updates couple magnitude and direction changes together
through a single low-rank delta, whereas full fine-tuning naturally decouples them.
DoRA's ablations show closer-to-full-fine-tuning learning dynamics at the same
parameter budget, at the cost of a small additional forward-pass overhead from
computing the magnitude norm — a real but usually acceptable trade for quality-critical
deployments.

## Initialization and the zero-start guarantee

`B` is initialized to zero (and `A` to small random values) specifically so that
`A @ B = 0` at step zero — the adapted model is mathematically identical to the base
model before any gradient steps happen. This matters for two practical reasons: it
guarantees training starts from a known-good point (no risk of an unlucky random init
immediately degrading generation quality), and it means adapter checkpoints saved very
early in training are safe fallbacks, unlike full fine-tuning where random-noise
gradients can be applied to the entire weight matrix from step one.

## Why LoRA composes poorly across multiple sequential adaptations

Merging adapter A into the base model, then training and merging adapter B on top,
is *not* equivalent to jointly training A and B, or to a single higher-rank adapter
covering both. Each merge folds a fixed low-rank correction into the full-rank weight,
after which further LoRA training sees a different effective starting point than either
adapter was trained against — errors and interference can compound across sequential
merges. This is why production systems that need many task-specific adaptations
typically keep adapters **unmerged and swappable** (loading whichever adapter matches
the request) rather than chaining merges, and why "continual LoRA fine-tuning" across
many tasks is an active research problem rather than a solved pattern.

## Trade-off summary

| Approach | Trainable params | Catastrophic forgetting | Peak quality on far-OOD tasks | Serving flexibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full fine-tuning | 100% | High risk | Best | One model per task |
| LoRA (standard) | ~0.1–1% | Low | Good on near-distribution tasks, weaker on far-OOD | Swappable adapters, shared base |
| LoRA + rsLoRA | ~0.1–1% | Low | Slightly better at high rank | Same as LoRA |
| DoRA | ~0.1–1% + small overhead | Low | Closer to full fine-tuning | Same as LoRA |
| QLoRA (LoRA + quantized base) | ~0.1–1% | Low | Comparable to LoRA | Requires dequant at merge time |

## The production decision in practice

Choosing LoRA over full fine-tuning is rarely purely an accuracy decision — it's an
infrastructure decision. If you need to serve many customer- or task-specific variants
of the same base model, LoRA's swappable adapters and shared-base-weight memory
footprint dominate even when full fine-tuning would nudge accuracy up a point or two.
Conversely, when a task requires the model to absorb genuinely new knowledge at scale
(e.g. domain-specific continual pretraining), teams increasingly reach for full
fine-tuning or higher-rank DoRA rather than fighting LoRA's inherent capacity ceiling.
