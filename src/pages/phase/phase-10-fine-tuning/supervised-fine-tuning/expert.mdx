## SFT as distribution-shifting, not knowledge injection

The most common misconception about SFT is treating it as a mechanism for teaching the
model new facts. In practice, SFT primarily reshapes the *output distribution* — which
of the many behaviors already latent in the pretrained model get surfaced by default —
rather than injecting substantial new knowledge. This is the core insight behind LIMA
(Zhou et al.): 1,000 carefully curated examples were sufficient to produce
competitive instruction-following behavior, because the underlying capability (factual
knowledge, reasoning patterns, language fluency) was already present from pretraining;
SFT's job was surfacing "answer helpfully and directly" as the default completion
style. The practical corollary: if your task genuinely requires new factual knowledge
the base model lacks, SFT alone is the wrong tool — you need continued pretraining or
retrieval augmentation, and SFT will mostly teach the model to *confidently state
things it doesn't actually know*, a direct contributor to hallucination.

## The loss-masking decision has second-order effects on calibration

Masking loss on prompt tokens (training only on assistant completions) is standard
practice, but it has an underappreciated consequence: the model never receives gradient
signal that would calibrate *how confidently* to answer based on prompt clarity or
ambiguity, because the loss landscape only ever "sees" clean completions attached to
whatever prompt preceded them. Some production pipelines counteract this by explicitly
including refusal/uncertainty examples ("I don't have enough information to answer
that") as first-class training targets — otherwise the SFT-trained model's default
posture skews toward always producing a confident, well-formatted answer, since that's
the only completion pattern loss ever rewarded during training.

## Packing efficiency vs. cross-contamination risk

Sequence packing (concatenating short examples to fill `max_length`) is a real
throughput win — often 2-4x fewer padding tokens processed — but naive packing without
proper attention masking lets tokens from example N attend to tokens from example N-1
within the same packed sequence, silently leaking context across unrelated examples.
Modern implementations (TRL's packing with `position_ids` reset, or explicit
block-diagonal attention masks) fix this, but it's a detail worth verifying rather than
assuming — a poorly implemented packing pipeline can quietly degrade quality in ways
that are hard to detect from loss curves alone, since the loss still goes down, just
against a subtly wrong objective.

## Epoch count as the primary catastrophic-forgetting lever

Unlike pretraining, where more epochs over more data is close to strictly better, SFT
exhibits a sharp quality/forgetting trade-off around epoch count. Empirically:

- **1 epoch**: often undertrained on smaller or more idiosyncratic datasets, especially
  for narrow domain adaptation.
- **2-3 epochs**: the common sweet spot for most instruction-tuning datasets of
  moderate size (thousands to low tens-of-thousands of examples).
- **5+ epochs**: measurable degradation on held-out general capability benchmarks
  becomes common, particularly with full fine-tuning and small/narrow datasets, as the
  model increasingly overfits to dataset-specific patterns at the expense of
  pretrained generality.

This is one of the more concrete arguments for PEFT (LoRA/QLoRA) over full fine-tuning
for SFT specifically: the rank constraint acts as an implicit regularizer that
meaningfully widens the safe epoch-count range before forgetting becomes visible.

## Trade-off summary

| Decision | Favors quality | Favors efficiency/safety |
|---|---|---|
| Full fine-tuning vs. LoRA/QLoRA | Full FT can reach slightly higher peak quality on large, diverse datasets | LoRA/QLoRA far cheaper, less prone to catastrophic forgetting |
| High epoch count | Better fit on the target dataset | Risk of forgetting general capability, overfitting to format |
| Packing enabled | Much higher training throughput | Requires correct cross-example attention masking |
| Large, broad dataset | Broader behavioral coverage | Harder to keep quality-controlled; noise dilutes signal |
| Small, curated dataset (LIMA-style) | Cleaner signal, easier to audit | May undercover edge cases and rare formats |

## Where SFT's limits show up downstream

SFT alone tends to plateau on tasks requiring the model to *rank* between several
plausible-but-imperfect responses — it only ever sees single "correct" targets, never
comparative signal about which of two decent answers is better. This gap is precisely
what preference-based methods ([RLHF](/phase/phase-10-fine-tuning/rlhf-concepts/expert)
and [DPO](/phase/phase-10-fine-tuning/dpo/expert)) are designed to close, which is why
production alignment pipelines almost universally run SFT first, then a preference
optimization stage — SFT establishes the response distribution, preference tuning
sharpens it.
