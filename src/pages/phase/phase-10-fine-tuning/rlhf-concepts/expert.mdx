## Reward model generalization is the pipeline's real bottleneck

The reward model is trained on a fixed, finite set of human preference comparisons, but
it's then used to score arbitrary policy-generated text during PPO — text the reward
model never saw during its own training. This distribution shift is the root cause of
most RLHF failure modes: as the policy updates, it drifts into regions of output space
the reward model was never trained to score reliably, and starts exploiting whatever
spurious correlations the reward model latched onto (length, specific phrasing,
superficial confidence markers) rather than genuine quality. This is why reward model
quality — diversity of training comparisons, calibration, resistance to length bias —
matters more to final RLHF outcomes than most of the PPO hyperparameters that get the
most tuning attention in practice.

## Why PPO's variance reduction machinery exists, and what it costs

Vanilla policy gradient has high variance because a single sampled trajectory's reward
is a noisy estimate of the true expected reward. PPO's value function (a fourth model,
alongside policy/reference/reward) estimates a baseline to subtract, reducing gradient
variance — but training a value function that itself needs to converge introduces its
own instability, particularly early in training when the value estimates are poor. Many
practical RLHF implementations report that value-function instability, not the policy
update itself, is the dominant source of training runs that need to be restarted or
have their KL coefficient re-tuned mid-run — a genuinely operational cost distinct from
the conceptual elegance of the PPO objective.

## The clip range and KL coefficient are coupled, not independent knobs

PPO's clipping (`epsilon`, typically 0.2) bounds how far a single update can move the
policy per step; the KL penalty coefficient bounds cumulative drift from the reference
model across the whole run. In practice these interact: a wide clip range with a weak
KL coefficient allows fast but potentially unstable large updates, while a tight clip
range with a strong KL coefficient can starve the policy of enough gradient signal to
meaningfully improve within a practical training budget. Teams often report needing to
retune both jointly rather than treating "KL coefficient" as the single dial for reward
hacking — over-tightening the clip range alone can just make training slow without
actually preventing reward exploitation.

## Adaptive KL control vs. fixed KL coefficients

A fixed KL coefficient (`init_kl_coef` held constant) assumes the "right" amount of
divergence is stable across the whole training run, which is rarely true in practice —
early training can tolerate more drift productively, while late training benefits from
tighter control to avoid degenerate collapse. **Adaptive KL controllers** (TRL supports
this via `AdaptiveKLController`) adjust the coefficient dynamically to target a desired
average KL divergence, trading a small amount of additional complexity for meaningfully
more stable long training runs — this is close to standard practice in serious RLHF
implementations rather than an optional refinement.

## Trade-off summary: RLHF (PPO) vs. its alternatives

| Dimension | RLHF (PPO) | DPO | Reward model + best-of-N (no RL) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Models needed simultaneously | 4 (policy, reference, reward, value) | 2 (policy, reference) | 2 (generator, reward model) |
| Training stability | Lower — sensitive to KL/clip tuning | Higher — closed-form loss, no RL loop | High — no gradient-based policy update at all |
| Compute cost | Highest | Lower | Lowest at train time, higher at inference time |
| Generalization beyond preference dataset | Better — reward model can score novel outputs | Weaker — implicitly tied to the fixed preference dataset's distribution | Depends entirely on reward model quality |
| Operational complexity | High | Moderate | Low, but scales poorly with N at inference |

## Where full RLHF still earns its complexity

Despite DPO's popularity, RLHF-with-PPO retains a real advantage: because the reward
model is a standalone, generalizing function, it can score *any* generated response,
including ones the policy produces after significant drift during training — DPO's
objective, by contrast, is only ever defined relative to the fixed pairs in its
training dataset, so it has no mechanism to actively explore and get feedback on novel
outputs during training. This is why frontier labs training for very open-ended,
long-horizon capabilities (rather than fine-tuning toward a fixed known-good
distribution) have historically retained PPO-based RLHF or hybrid approaches, even as
DPO dominates as the practical default for most applied fine-tuning teams.
