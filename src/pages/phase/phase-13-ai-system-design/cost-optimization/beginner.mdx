## Every word costs money

Unlike a typical web app where serving a page costs almost nothing extra
per user, calling an LLM costs real money **every single time**, and the
cost scales with how much text goes in and comes out. Providers charge per
token (roughly a word-piece) — both the tokens you send (the prompt) and the
tokens the model generates (the response). A chatty conversation with a long
history and a long answer can cost meaningfully more than a short,
to-the-point exchange.

## Why this matters more as you scale

If one request costs a fraction of a cent, that sounds trivial — until your
product has a million users sending several requests a day. Costs that look
negligible per request add up fast at scale, which is why cost isn't
something you bolt on later; it shapes real product decisions from day one,
like how much conversation history to resend, or which model tier to use
for which feature.

## Three simple levers

- **Use a smaller/cheaper model when you can.** Not every task needs the
  most powerful (and most expensive) model — a simple classification task
  doesn't need the same model as complex reasoning.
- **Don't send more context than you need.** Trimming unnecessary
  conversation history or irrelevant documents reduces input tokens, which
  directly reduces cost.
- **Cache repeated work.** If the same question gets asked often, don't pay
  to regenerate the same answer every time (see the "Caching" topic in this
  phase).

## Try it yourself

A support chatbot resends the entire conversation history on every message.
After 20 back-and-forth messages, is the 21st message cheaper, about the
same, or more expensive to process than the 1st message was?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

More expensive. Because the whole history gets resent every time, message 21
carries the weight of 20 previous exchanges as input tokens, while message 1
had none. The per-message cost grows as the conversation grows — which is
exactly why long-running conversations are a real cost concern, not just a
performance one, and why production systems often summarize or trim old
history rather than resending it forever.
</details>
