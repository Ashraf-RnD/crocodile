## Statistical significance is not the same as business significance

A drift test with enough samples will nearly always find *some* `p < 0.05`
result across dozens of monitored features — this is the multiple-comparisons
problem, and naive drift monitoring that alerts on any single significant
test produces alert fatigue that erodes trust in the whole system. Two
mitigations matter in practice:

- **Correct for multiple comparisons** (Bonferroni or Benjamini-Hochberg)
  when testing many features simultaneously, or use an aggregate score (PSI
  averaged/weighted across features) rather than alerting per-feature.
- **Separate statistical detection from business materiality.** A feature
  can drift significantly (`p < 0.001`) while causing negligible actual
  prediction degradation, because the model's sensitivity to that feature is
  low. The right escalation signal is drift *combined with* estimated
  performance impact (via CBPE-style methods), not drift alone — alerting
  purely on statistical drift without an impact estimate trains on-call
  engineers to ignore the alerts.

## Concept drift is fundamentally harder to detect than data drift

Data drift (input distribution shift) is directly observable from incoming
requests. Concept drift (the input→output relationship changing) is only
observable once ground-truth labels arrive — and by definition, if you had
instant labels you often wouldn't need the model in the first place. This
creates a structural detection lag that differs by domain:

- **Fast-label domains** (ad click prediction — label available in minutes):
  concept drift is detectable near real-time via rolling accuracy metrics
- **Slow-label domains** (loan default prediction — label available in
  months): concept drift can only be inferred indirectly, via proxy signals
  like prediction confidence calibration drift or input drift correlated
  with historically-known drift episodes, since waiting for ground truth
  means discovering the problem long after it started

Teams in slow-label domains often invest heavily in proxy/indirect drift
signals precisely because the alternative — waiting for real labels — means
a model can silently degrade for months before anyone notices.

## Drift thresholds need a decision policy, not just a number

Setting `PSI > 0.25` as an automatic retraining trigger without further
context creates two distinct failure modes:

- **Over-triggering**: seasonal, expected, recurring shifts (e.g. retail
  traffic patterns around a holiday) repeatedly trigger unnecessary
  retraining, burning GPU budget and creating "alert fatigue" that leads
  teams to eventually just raise the threshold — silently reducing
  sensitivity to real drift.
- **Under-triggering**: a slow, gradual drift that never crosses a fixed
  threshold in any single measurement window can still accumulate into
  serious model degradation over months — cumulative drift detection
  (tracking drift trend over time, not just point-in-time snapshots) catches
  this where a fixed-threshold snapshot test doesn't.

Mature drift monitoring systems distinguish **seasonal/cyclical drift**
(expected, should not trigger retraining) from **structural drift**
(a genuine, sustained shift) using techniques like comparing against the
same period a year prior, or decomposing the time series before applying a
drift test — treating every distributional change as equally alarming is a
common source of both false alarms and missed real ones.

## Embeddings and drift in unstructured/high-dimensional inputs

For models consuming text, images, or embeddings, classical univariate
statistical tests (KS test per feature) don't apply directly — the relevant
drift signal is in the joint, high-dimensional distribution. Common
approaches:

- **Embedding-space drift**: project inputs through the model's own
  embedding layer, then measure distributional shift in that lower-dimensional
  space (e.g. Maximum Mean Discrepancy, or drift in centroid distance)
- **Model-based drift proxies**: monitor the trained model's own prediction
  confidence/entropy distribution over time — a rising share of
  low-confidence predictions is often a more actionable drift signal for
  unstructured inputs than any statistical test on raw pixels or tokens

This is a meaningfully different toolkit from tabular drift detection, and
teams that only bring tabular-style statistical tests to an LLM or vision
pipeline typically miss the drift signals that actually matter for those
systems.

## Trade-offs across drift detection strategies

| Approach | Detects | Lag | Cost | Failure mode if misapplied |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Univariate statistical tests (KS, chi-squared) | Data drift, per-feature | Fast (no labels needed) | Low | Alert fatigue from multiple-comparisons noise |
| PSI / aggregate scores | Data drift, feature-set level | Fast | Low | Masks which specific feature actually shifted |
| Label-based performance monitoring | Concept drift, ground truth | Slow (label-dependent) | Low (once labels exist) | Too late in slow-label domains |
| Confidence-based estimation (CBPE, NannyML) | Concept drift proxy, no labels needed | Fast | Moderate | Can misestimate under severe concept drift itself |
| Embedding/model-based drift | Unstructured input drift | Fast | Higher (requires model internals) | Overkill for simple tabular features |

No single method is sufficient alone — production drift monitoring
typically layers fast, cheap data-drift signals for early warning with
slower, more expensive concept-drift/performance-based signals for
confirmation, because optimizing purely for detection speed produces false
alarms, while optimizing purely for accuracy produces dangerously slow
detection.
