## Chunking as an information-loss problem

Every chunking strategy is a lossy compression decision: you're deciding what context
to keep next to what text, and what context to discard. The core failure mode is that
a chunk, read in isolation, often loses the referent it needs to be retrievable or
useful — "the policy took effect in that year" means nothing without knowing which
policy and which year, even if the sentence was perfectly clear inside its original
paragraph. Chunk size and boundary choice are really proxies for a harder question:
*how much disambiguating context does this piece of text need to carry on its own?*

## Contextual chunking: fixing the isolation problem directly

Rather than hoping overlap or larger chunks preserve enough context, **contextual
retrieval** (popularized by Anthropic) prepends a short LLM-generated summary of each
chunk's place in the document before embedding it:

```python
def contextualize_chunk(llm, full_document, chunk):
    prompt = f"""<document>{full_document}</document>
Here is a chunk from this document:
<chunk>{chunk}</chunk>
Give a short, succinct context (1-2 sentences) to situate this chunk within the
overall document, for the purpose of improving search retrieval. Answer only with
the context."""
    context = llm.invoke(prompt).content
    return f"{context}\n\n{chunk}"

# "This chunk is from the Q3 2023 SEC filing for Acme Corp, discussing revenue growth
#  in the enterprise segment.\n\n[original chunk text]"
```

This adds an LLM call per chunk at ingestion time (expensive, but a one-time,
offline cost, and cacheable with prompt caching against the shared full-document
context) in exchange for a substantial gain in retrieval accuracy — Anthropic reported
roughly a 35% reduction in failed retrievals when combined with BM25 hybrid search,
and closer to 49% when combined with reranking too. The trade-off is squarely
ingestion-time compute cost against query-time retrieval quality.

## Chunk size is a bias-variance trade-off, not a single "right" number

| Chunk size | Retrieval precision | Context sufficiency | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Very small (~50-100 tokens) | High — narrow, focused embeddings match queries precisely | Low — LLM often lacks context to answer fully | "Needle" retrieved but answer is incomplete or wrong without neighbors |
| Medium (~300-500 tokens) | Balanced | Balanced | Reasonable default; still splits mid-idea sometimes |
| Large (~1000+ tokens) | Low — embedding is an average over multiple topics, dilutes similarity to any single query | High — LLM has ample context | Relevant chunk buried under irrelevant text, may get dropped by reranker's top-k cutoff |
| Whole-document | Very low — embedding barely distinguishes intent | Maximum | Effectively defeats the purpose of retrieval; you're back to long-context stuffing |

The practical resolution most production systems converge on is **decoupling
retrieval granularity from generation granularity**: embed and index small, precise
chunks (child chunks), but when a child chunk is retrieved, pass its parent section or
neighboring chunks to the LLM. LlamaIndex's `AutoMergingRetriever` and the
"parent-document retriever" pattern in LangChain implement exactly this — search
against leaves, generate with the branch.

## Structural vs. semantic vs. agentic chunking, and when each earns its cost

- **Structural** (Markdown headers, HTML DOM, code AST) is nearly free at ingestion
  time and highly reliable *when the document format is trustworthy* — but breaks down
  on scanned PDFs, inconsistent Word exports, or auto-generated HTML with no
  meaningful structure.
- **Semantic** (embedding-similarity breakpoints) recovers meaningful boundaries in
  unstructured prose but costs an embedding call per sentence at ingestion and is
  sensitive to the breakpoint threshold — too aggressive and you fragment excessively,
  too lax and you're back to large undifferentiated chunks.
- **Agentic/LLM-driven chunking** (asking an LLM to propose chunk boundaries directly,
  or to extract self-contained propositions) gives the highest-quality boundaries but
  is the most expensive per document and introduces LLM-specific failure modes (an LLM
  can hallucinate boundaries or silently drop content) that require validation against
  the source text.

The right choice per corpus is rarely "use the fanciest technique everywhere" — most
production systems mix strategies: structural splitting for well-formatted docs (API
references, internal wikis), a fallback semantic or recursive splitter for messy
inputs, and contextual chunking layered on top of whichever splitter is used, since it
addresses a different failure mode (isolation) than any splitter alone.

## Chunk boundaries interact with your reranker and eval loop, not just your retriever

Changing chunk size silently changes the recall@k your reranker sees, because smaller
chunks mean more total chunks compete for the same top-k slots — a corpus that used to
produce 3 relevant chunks in the top 10 might now produce 8 near-duplicate fragments
of the same 3 answers, crowding out other relevant content. This is why chunking
strategy should be evaluated end-to-end (retrieval recall *and* final answer
correctness on a held-out QA set), not chosen in isolation and assumed to be a solved,
independent step of the pipeline — a chunking change that looks like an improvement in
raw embedding similarity scores can still regress final answer quality if it
interacts badly with your top-k and reranking configuration.
