## Model landscape comparison

| Model | Architecture | Open weights | License (best tier) | Guidance distilled | Strong point |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SDXL | UNet | Yes | Permissive (CreativeML OpenRAIL++) | No (some Turbo variants) | Mature ecosystem, LoRA/ControlNet support |
| SD3 | MMDiT transformer | Yes (medium size) | Restrictive for commercial use at scale | No | Strong prompt adherence |
| FLUX.1-schnell | MMDiT + rectified flow | Yes | Apache 2.0 (commercial OK) | Yes | Fast, legally clean for products |
| FLUX.1-dev | MMDiT + rectified flow | Yes | Non-commercial | Yes | Highest open-weight quality |
| Midjourney | Undisclosed | No | Hosted service only | Unknown | Strong aesthetic default, no self-hosting |

FLUX's licensing split (schnell fully open, dev restricted, pro API-only) is a
deliberate commercial strategy as much as a technical one — it lets Black Forest Labs
give away a genuinely useful, unrestricted model (driving adoption and ecosystem
tooling) while keeping their best quality behind a paid API, similar to how other
foundation model companies segment free/open vs paid tiers.

## Guidance distillation: what schnell actually gives up

Guidance-distilled models like `schnell` are trained to bake classifier-free guidance
behavior directly into the model weights, letting inference skip the extra forward
pass CFG normally requires — this is *why* it can generate in 1-4 steps at
`guidance_scale=0.0`. The trade-off is a narrower quality ceiling and somewhat reduced
prompt diversity across different seeds compared to `dev`: distillation optimizes for
"good result fast," which tends to compress the range of plausible outputs the model
will produce for a given prompt, a mild form of mode collapse relative to the
non-distilled model it was distilled from.

## Running FLUX on consumer hardware

FLUX's full-precision weights (the transformer alone is ~12B parameters for
dev/schnell) don't fit comfortably on consumer GPU VRAM without intervention. Common
production/hobbyist workarounds:

```python
pipe = FluxPipeline.from_pretrained(
    "black-forest-labs/FLUX.1-schnell",
    torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
)
pipe.enable_model_cpu_offload()   # keep only active submodule on GPU
# or: load a quantized (e.g. 8-bit/NF4) version of the transformer weights
```

`enable_model_cpu_offload()` keeps the text encoders, transformer, and VAE mostly on
CPU RAM and only moves each onto the GPU when it's actively needed for that step,
trading generation speed for the ability to run on GPUs with as little as 8-12GB VRAM
— the standard technique that makes FLUX practical outside of data-center-grade GPUs.

## Why FLUX pushed the field toward transformers over UNets

FLUX's success (alongside SD3) reinforced a broader architectural shift already
happening across diffusion research: transformer backbones scale more predictably
with additional data and compute than convolutional UNets did, mirroring the same
scaling-law dynamics that drove the field from CNNs to transformers in vision
classification years earlier. This is less about transformers being intrinsically
"better at images" and more that they inherit a much larger body of scaling and
training-stability knowledge from LLM research, which shows up directly in how
reliably these newer models improve with more training compute compared to earlier
UNet-based diffusion models.
