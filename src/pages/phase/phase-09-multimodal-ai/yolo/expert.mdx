## One-stage vs two-stage vs transformer detectors

| Approach | Example | Speed | Accuracy (small objects) | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One-stage, anchor-free | YOLOv8/v11 | Very fast (real-time) | Good | Real-time video, edge devices |
| Two-stage | Faster R-CNN | Slower | Best on small/overlapping objects | Offline high-accuracy analysis |
| Transformer (set prediction) | DETR / RT-DETR | Moderate | Good, no NMS needed | Research, increasingly production |

YOLO's speed advantage comes directly from being single-pass and anchor-free: it skips
the two-stage "propose regions, then classify each region" pipeline of Faster R-CNN
entirely, and skips NMS-sensitive anchor matching by predicting boxes directly. The
trade-off shows up on **small, densely packed objects** (a crowd of distant people, a
shelf of small products) where two-stage detectors' region-proposal step still tends to
edge out YOLO's accuracy, though the gap has narrowed significantly through YOLOv8-v11.

## Why NMS is a production liability, not just a detail

Non-Maximum Suppression relies on a fixed IoU (intersection-over-union) threshold to
decide which overlapping boxes are "the same object." This breaks down predictably:

- **Dense scenes** (overlapping people in a crowd) — legitimate separate detections get
  suppressed as duplicates.
- **Class-specific tuning** — a threshold tuned for cars (large, well-separated) often
  over-suppresses smaller, closer objects like faces.

This is exactly why transformer-based detectors like DETR/RT-DETR frame detection as a
direct **set prediction** problem (bipartite matching during training) — eliminating
NMS entirely removes this failure mode, at the cost of more complex, slower training.

## Deployment: quantization and TensorRT

A YOLO model trained in float32 PyTorch is rarely what ships to production, especially
on edge devices (cameras, drones, embedded boards). The standard path:

```python
model.export(format="engine", half=True, int8=False)  # TensorRT FP16 export
```

FP16 (half precision) typically costs negligible accuracy for a 2x+ inference speedup
on NVIDIA GPUs. INT8 quantization goes further (roughly 4x speedup over FP32) but
requires a calibration dataset and does cost measurable accuracy — teams pick INT8 only
when latency/power budgets are truly tight (embedded/mobile), not by default.

## Confidence threshold is a business decision, not a hyperparameter

The default `conf=0.25` in Ultralytics is a starting point, not a correct answer. A
security system flagging intruders should bias toward a lower threshold (catch more,
tolerate false positives triggering a human review) — a manufacturing defect detector
gating physical part rejection should bias much higher (a false positive throws away a
good part; a false negative ships a defect). This threshold is usually the single
highest-leverage tuning knob in a YOLO deployment, and it should be set by looking at
a precision-recall curve on validation data, not left at the library default.
