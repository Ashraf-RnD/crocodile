## The problem before this paper

In a standard ("dense") neural network, every single parameter is used to
process every single input. Want a more capable model? You add more
parameters — but every input now costs more compute, because it still has to
pass through all of them. This links model capacity and computational cost
together in lockstep: you cannot make a model "know more" without also making
every prediction more expensive, which puts a hard ceiling on how large you
can practically scale a dense model.

## The core idea, in plain language

The Mixture of Experts (MoE) idea breaks that link. Instead of one dense
network that processes everything, you build many smaller specialized
sub-networks — "experts" — and add a small **router** (or "gate") that looks
at each input and decides which handful of experts should process it. Only
those selected experts do any work for that particular input; the rest sit
idle. Total model capacity (the sum of all experts) can grow enormously,
while the compute cost per input stays tied to just the few experts that
were actually activated.

A useful analogy: think of a large hospital with dozens of specialists —
cardiologists, dermatologists, orthopedists. A patient doesn't see every
specialist in the building; a receptionist (the router) looks at their
symptoms and sends them to the two or three most relevant specialists. The
hospital's total expertise (capacity) is enormous, but any individual visit
only costs the time of the few specialists actually consulted, not the whole
staff.

## Why it mattered

The original 2017 paper demonstrated MoE layers scaling a model to over 130
billion parameters while keeping the compute cost per input close to that of
a much smaller dense model — a decoupling of "how much the model knows" from
"how much compute answering costs" that dense architectures simply couldn't
offer. That decoupling is precisely why, years later, MoE became the
architecture of choice for building today's largest and most
capability-per-dollar-efficient language models, including several covered
elsewhere in this phase.
