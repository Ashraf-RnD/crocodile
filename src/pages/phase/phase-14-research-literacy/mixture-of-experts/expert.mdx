## What the paper actually contributed vs. prior work

Mixture of Experts as a general machine learning concept predates deep
learning by decades (Jacobs et al., 1991). Shazeer et al.'s 2017 contribution
was making sparse gating **practical inside deep neural networks trained with
backpropagation**, at a scale (thousands of experts, 130B+ total parameters)
no prior work had attempted. The core technical obstacles it solved: making
the discrete top-k selection differentiable enough to train end-to-end, and
introducing the load-balancing auxiliary loss to prevent gate collapse onto a
small expert subset. Its explicit framing — decoupling total model capacity
from per-example compute cost — became the conceptual foundation nearly all
subsequent MoE language models (including Switch Transformer, GShard,
Mixtral, and the DeepSeek/Kimi models covered elsewhere in this phase) build
on directly.

## Limitations

- **Training instability.** Sparse routing decisions are inherently
  discontinuous, and MoE training runs are empirically more prone to loss
  spikes and divergence than equivalent dense models — a persistent
  engineering challenge that later papers (DeepSeek-V3's auxiliary-loss-free
  balancing, for instance) specifically targeted.
- **Distributed training communication overhead.** Because different experts
  typically live on different accelerators, routing tokens to their assigned
  expert requires all-to-all communication across devices — this
  cross-device traffic can become the actual bottleneck at scale, not compute
  itself, which is why efficient MoE implementations invest heavily in
  routing/communication engineering (e.g. GShard's sharding strategies).
- **Expert underutilization and the load-balancing trade-off.** The
  auxiliary load-balancing loss is itself an approximation — forcing more
  uniform routing can conflict with letting the gate route tokens to
  wherever they'd genuinely be handled best, a tension between load
  efficiency and specialization quality that later routing schemes continued
  to refine.
- **Larger memory footprint despite lower compute.** All experts' parameters
  must typically be resident in memory (across a device cluster) even though
  only a few are used per token — MoE reduces FLOPs per token, not total
  memory required to serve the model, which is a frequently
  misunderstood trade-off.

## What came after

- **GShard (2020)** scaled MoE Transformers to hundreds of billions of
  parameters for machine translation, contributing practical sharding and
  routing strategies for distributed training.
- **Switch Transformer (2021)** simplified routing to top-1 and scaled to over
  a trillion parameters, showing simpler routing could be both more stable
  and more efficient than the original's denser gating.
- **Mixtral of Experts (2024)** brought MoE to the open-weight ecosystem at a
  practical, widely-deployable scale (8 experts, top-2 routing, ~47B total /
  ~13B active parameters), demonstrating strong quality-per-active-parameter
  efficiency outside a frontier lab's exclusive infrastructure.
- **Fine-grained expert architectures with shared experts** (DeepSeekMoE,
  used in DeepSeek-V3, covered elsewhere in this phase) split experts into
  many smaller, more specialized units plus a small number of "always-on"
  shared experts that capture common knowledge — improving specialization
  while reducing redundant capacity across experts.
- **Auxiliary-loss-free load balancing** (also introduced in DeepSeek-V3)
  replaced the classic auxiliary loss with a bias-adjustment mechanism, aiming
  to balance load without the training-signal interference an auxiliary loss
  can introduce.

## How it's actually used in production today

MoE is now the architecture of choice for essentially every lab pursuing
maximum capability per unit of inference compute at the frontier: Mixtral,
DeepSeek-V3, Kimi K2, and (widely believed, though not officially confirmed
for all cases) some of the largest closed models all use sparse MoE layers.
The appeal in production is direct: users get access to a very large total
knowledge base while the serving cost per request tracks the much smaller
*active* parameter count, not the full model size — a decisive economic
advantage at the scale frontier labs now operate.

## Comparison: dense Transformer vs. Mixture-of-Experts Transformer

| Dimension | Dense Transformer | Mixture-of-Experts Transformer |
|---|---|---|
| Parameters used per token | All of them | Only the routed subset (e.g. 2 of many experts) |
| Total capacity vs. compute cost | Tightly coupled | Decoupled — capacity can scale far ahead of per-token compute |
| Training stability | Well understood, stable | More prone to instability; requires load-balancing techniques |
| Memory to serve the model | Proportional to active compute | Must hold all experts, even though few run per token |
| Distributed training complexity | Standard data/model parallelism | Requires expert parallelism and all-to-all routing communication |
| Typical use case today | Smaller/mid-size models, simpler deployment | Frontier-scale models optimizing capability per inference dollar |
