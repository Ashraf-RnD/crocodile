## The problem before this paper

BERT and GPT-2 had already shown that pretraining a Transformer on huge amounts of
text produced useful general-purpose language understanding. But using that
pretrained model for a specific task — sentiment analysis, translation,
summarization — still normally required a **fine-tuning** step: collecting a
labeled dataset for that exact task and updating the model's weights on it. That
meant a new dataset and a new training run for every new task, even though the
model had already "read" nearly the entire internet.

## The core idea, in plain language

GPT-3's authors asked: what if a language model, made large enough, could learn a
new task just from a few examples typed directly into the prompt — no weight
updates, no separate training run at all? They called this **in-context
learning**. You simply show the model a handful of examples of the task
(input/output pairs) as text, followed by a new input, and it predicts a
plausible output by pattern-matching against the examples it just read.

A useful analogy: instead of sending a new employee to a multi-week training
course every time you need them to do a slightly different task (fine-tuning),
you just show them two or three worked examples on a sticky note and let them
figure out the pattern (in-context learning). GPT-3 scaled the underlying model
from GPT-2's 1.5 billion parameters to 175 billion, and the striking discovery
was that this "read a few examples, then generalize" ability got dramatically
better as the model got bigger — it wasn't explicitly trained to do this, it
emerged from scale.

## Why it mattered

GPT-3 demonstrated that scale itself — more parameters, more data, more compute —
could substitute for task-specific training, at least for many tasks. That
observation reframed how the field thought about building AI systems: instead of
training a new model per task, you train one very large model once and *prompt*
it differently for each use case. This is the direct ancestor of how you interact
with ChatGPT, Claude, and virtually every modern LLM product today — through
natural-language prompts, not custom training runs.
