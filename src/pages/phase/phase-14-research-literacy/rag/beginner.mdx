## The problem before this paper

A language model's knowledge is baked into its weights during training and
frozen the moment training ends. That creates two persistent problems: the
model can't know about anything that happened after its training cutoff, and
when it doesn't actually know something, it tends to confidently generate a
plausible-sounding but wrong answer — a **hallucination** — rather than
admitting uncertainty, because generating fluent text is exactly what it was
trained to do, with or without the right facts to back it up. Retraining a huge
model just to update its knowledge is far too expensive to do routinely.

## The core idea, in plain language

The RAG paper's answer: instead of relying purely on what the model
memorized during training (**parametric** knowledge), give it access to an
external, searchable collection of documents, and let it **retrieve** relevant
passages at the moment it needs to answer a question — then generate its
answer conditioned on those retrieved passages, alongside its own trained
knowledge.

A useful analogy: think of the difference between a closed-book exam and an
open-book exam. A model without retrieval is taking a closed-book exam — it
must recall everything from memory, and if its memory is wrong or incomplete,
so is its answer. A model with retrieval gets to open a relevant reference book
to the right page before answering — it can look up the actual fact instead of
guessing from memory, and if the underlying document collection is updated,
its "knowledge" updates immediately, with no retraining required.

## Why it mattered

RAG showed that combining a retriever with a generator measurably reduced
factual errors and let a single model answer questions about far more
information than it could ever memorize in its weights alone — including
information it never saw during training at all, as long as it's in the
retrievable document collection. That combination — up-to-date, inspectable,
swappable knowledge plus a fluent generator — is exactly why RAG became the
default architecture for building chatbots and assistants over private or
frequently changing data (internal company documents, recent news, personal
files) rather than fine-tuning a model on that data directly.
