_This lesson is a critical analysis of the LoRA paper as research — its
contribution, limitations, and legacy. The practical "how do I fine-tune a
model with LoRA" guide lives in Phase 10: Fine-tuning; the two are
complementary, not duplicates._

## What the paper actually contributed vs. prior work

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) wasn't a new goal in 2021 — **adapter
layers** (Houlsby et al., 2019) inserted small trainable bottleneck layers
between frozen Transformer sub-layers, and **prefix-tuning** / **prompt
tuning** prepended trainable "soft" tokens to the input. Both reduced trainable
parameter count substantially versus full fine-tuning. LoRA's distinguishing
contribution was structural: because its update is expressed as an *additive*
low-rank term to an *existing* weight matrix (`W0 + BA`) rather than a new
layer inserted into the forward pass, it can be **merged into the frozen
weights after training**, producing a model with the exact same architecture
and inference latency as the base model. Adapter layers, by contrast,
permanently add sequential computation to every forward pass; prefix-tuning
permanently consumes part of the usable context window. LoRA's zero-overhead
merge property, combined with matching or exceeding adapter/prefix-tuning
quality on the benchmarks tested, is what the paper's ablations were built to
demonstrate.

## Limitations

- **Doesn't reduce training memory as much as it seems.** Because the frozen
  base model's activations still must be stored for backpropagation to reach
  the trainable `A`/`B` matrices, LoRA saves significantly on **optimizer
  state** and **gradient** memory, but the full model still needs to be loaded
  in a trainable-precision format (originally fp16/bf16) — this is exactly the
  gap QLoRA (covered in its own lesson) was built to close by quantizing the
  frozen base to 4-bit.
- **Rank selection is an empirical hyperparameter, not a derived quantity.**
  The paper shows small ranks work well on its specific benchmarks, but the
  "right" rank is task- and model-dependent, and choosing too small a rank can
  measurably cap achievable quality on harder adaptation tasks.
- **Uniform rank/placement is a simplification.** Applying the same rank to
  every targeted layer treats all layers as equally important for adaptation,
  which later work argued is suboptimal.
- **A gap to full fine-tuning still exists on some tasks**, particularly ones
  requiring substantial new knowledge injection rather than behavioral
  steering of existing knowledge — low-rank updates are fundamentally limited
  in how much new information they can encode.

## What came after

- **QLoRA (2023)** combined LoRA with 4-bit quantization of the frozen base
  model, addressing LoRA's remaining memory bottleneck and enabling fine-tuning
  of 65B-parameter models on a single consumer GPU.
- **AdaLoRA** made rank allocation adaptive per layer/matrix based on
  importance scores during training, rather than a fixed uniform rank.
- **DoRA (Weight-Decomposed LoRA)** decomposes the pretrained weight into a
  magnitude component and a directional component, applying low-rank updates
  only to direction — narrowing the quality gap to full fine-tuning further.
- **Multi-LoRA serving systems** (S-LoRA, LoRAX, Punica) exploit the fact that
  many small LoRA adapters can share one frozen base model in GPU memory,
  enabling a single deployment to serve thousands of different fine-tuned
  "personalities" or task adapters concurrently by swapping only the small
  `A`/`B` matrices per request — a capability full fine-tuning could never
  offer economically.

## How it's actually used in production today

LoRA (or a close descendant like QLoRA or DoRA) is the default fine-tuning
method for open-weight models in essentially every production or research
setting outside of full frontier-lab pretraining. Its zero-inference-overhead
merge property makes it attractive even when memory isn't the binding
constraint — you get most of full fine-tuning's quality with dramatically
smaller checkpoints (megabytes instead of gigabytes), which is what makes
serving many customized model variants from a single base model economically
viable, a pattern now common in multi-tenant LLM platforms.

## Comparison: full fine-tuning vs. adapter layers vs. LoRA

| Dimension | Full fine-tuning | Adapter layers (2019) | LoRA (2021) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trainable parameters | 100% of model | Small (added layers) | Small (~0.01-1%) |
| Extra inference latency | None (same architecture) | Yes — permanent extra layers | None after merging into base weights |
| Storage per fine-tuned task | Full model copy | Small adapter weights | Small adapter weights (mergeable) |
| Can serve many tasks from one base model in memory | Impractical at scale | Yes, with latency cost | Yes, near-zero cost |
| Quality vs. full fine-tuning | Reference point | Slightly below, in general | Competitive, task-dependent |
