## The problem before this paper

Training a frontier-capability language model was widely assumed to require
budgets in the tens or hundreds of millions of dollars — massive GPU clusters
run for months — putting genuinely competitive model training out of reach
for all but a handful of well-funded labs. Efficiency techniques existed
individually (Mixture of Experts, lower-precision training, better attention
mechanisms), but no released frontier-scale model had stacked enough of them
together, and pushed each one far enough, to fundamentally change the
economics of getting there.

## The core idea, in plain language

DeepSeek-V3 didn't invent a wholly new paradigm — it combined and pushed
several engineering efficiency techniques much further than prior work,
across nearly every layer of the training stack: a smarter, more
memory-efficient attention mechanism, a more specialized Mixture-of-Experts
design, extremely low-precision (8-bit) training arithmetic where earlier
frontier models had insisted on higher precision, and a training objective
that gets more useful signal out of each token processed. The team reported
training a 671-billion-parameter model (with only 37 billion parameters
"active" per token, thanks to MoE — see the Mixture of Experts lesson) for
roughly $5.5 million in compute — remarkably low next to the widely assumed
cost of frontier training at the time — while reaching benchmark performance
competitive with leading closed models.

A useful analogy: imagine most car manufacturers assume you need a massive
factory and huge budget to build a genuinely fast car. DeepSeek-V3 is like a
smaller team that, instead of building a bigger factory, carefully
re-engineered the engine, the fuel system, and the assembly line itself —
each individually a modest improvement — and the combination let them build a
comparably fast car for a fraction of the assumed cost.

## Why it mattered

DeepSeek-V3's release (and the reasoning model built on top of it,
DeepSeek-R1, shortly after) triggered a widely reported reassessment — dubbed
by some commentators the "DeepSeek moment" — of assumptions about how much
compute and money were strictly necessary to reach frontier-level model
quality, and it was released as **open weights**, letting anyone inspect,
self-host, or build on it. It demonstrated that clever systems engineering
could substitute, to a meaningful degree, for sheer compute spending — a
genuinely consequential result for how the rest of the industry thought about
the cost curve of AI progress.
