## The problem before this paper

_Note: this lesson analyzes the DPO **paper** — what it contributed and how it
works technically. For a practical how-to guide on running DPO fine-tuning,
see the DPO lesson under Phase 10: Fine-tuning._

Making a language model helpful and safe usually requires more than just
predicting the next word — it requires teaching it *which* of several
plausible responses humans actually prefer. The standard approach,
**Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**, does this in three
stages: train a separate **reward model** to score responses the way humans
would, then use a reinforcement learning algorithm (typically PPO) to nudge
the language model toward generating higher-reward responses. This works, but
it's notoriously complicated: you need to train and maintain two models
(the policy and the reward model), the RL training loop is unstable and
sensitive to hyperparameters, and getting it right required real
RL expertise that most fine-tuning teams didn't have.

## The core idea, in plain language

DPO's authors made a mathematical observation: the entire RLHF pipeline's
end goal — a policy that assigns higher probability to preferred responses and
lower probability to dispreferred ones — can be reached **directly**, using a
simple classification-style loss on pairs of (preferred, dispreferred)
responses, with no separate reward model and no reinforcement learning loop at
all.

A useful analogy: RLHF is like training a chef by first hiring a food critic
to build a detailed scoring rubric (the reward model), then having the chef
repeatedly cook dishes and adjust based on the critic's scores through trial
and error (reinforcement learning) — a slow, indirect feedback loop. DPO is
like skipping the critic and rubric entirely, and instead directly showing the
chef pairs of dishes — "diners preferred this one over that one" — letting
them adjust their technique straight from the comparisons. Same end goal,
dramatically simpler path to it.

## Why it mattered

DPO reaches comparable alignment quality to RLHF on the benchmarks the paper
tested, using a training setup as simple as ordinary supervised fine-tuning —
no reward model to train and discard, no unstable RL loop, no extensive
RL-specific hyperparameter tuning. That simplicity made preference-based
alignment accessible to far more teams, and DPO (or one of its close variants)
quickly became the default way open-weight models are aligned to human
preferences.
