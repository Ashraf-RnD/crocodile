_This lesson is a critical analysis of the DPO paper. The practical
"how do I actually run DPO fine-tuning" guide lives in Phase 10:
Fine-tuning; the two are complementary._

## What the paper actually contributed vs. prior work

RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017; applied to LLMs by InstructGPT, 2022) had
already established that optimizing a KL-constrained reward objective against
human preferences produces well-aligned models. DPO's contribution wasn't a
new alignment *goal* — it targets the exact same KL-constrained
reward-maximization objective as RLHF — but a new, exact **reparameterization**
showing that objective's optimal solution can be reached by directly
optimizing the policy's own log-probability ratios on preference pairs,
without ever instantiating a separate reward model or running an RL rollout
loop. The paper proves this reparameterization is mathematically equivalent to
the RLHF objective under the Bradley-Terry preference model, not merely an
approximation — a theoretically grounded simplification rather than an
empirical shortcut, backed up with experiments showing DPO matches or exceeds
PPO-based RLHF on several tasks while being far simpler and more stable to
train.

## Limitations

- **Purely offline.** DPO trains on a fixed, pre-collected preference dataset
  and never samples fresh responses from the policy during training — RLHF's
  online rollouts let the reward model evaluate the *current* policy's actual
  outputs as it changes, giving it a moving target to correct against; DPO's
  reference distribution is fixed at the start, which can make it less robust
  to distribution shift as the policy diverges from its initial behavior over
  training.
- **Sensitive to the reference model.** Since DPO's implicit reward is
  defined *relative to* `π_ref`, a poorly chosen or poorly calibrated
  reference model can bias the entire training signal — the reference isn't
  incidental, it's baked directly into the objective.
- **Can overfit to the preference data's specific distribution**, including
  its idiosyncrasies or biases in how responses were compared, more directly
  than a separately trained reward model (which can sometimes generalize
  better past the exact seen comparisons).
- **Doesn't inherently support more nuanced feedback signals** like scalar
  ratings, multi-turn credit assignment, or process-level supervision
  (rewarding intermediate reasoning steps, not just final outputs) as
  naturally as reward-model-based RL — this gap motivated the follow-up work
  below.

## What came after

- **IPO (Identity Preference Optimization)** addressed a specific overfitting
  failure mode in DPO's loss where, with certain data, the sigmoid loss can
  push probabilities toward extremes rather than converging to a sensible
  preference margin, by proposing a bounded alternative loss.
- **KTO (Kahneman-Tversky Optimization)** removed the requirement for paired
  preference data entirely, using only a binary "good/bad" signal per
  response (grounded in prospect theory), which is often cheaper to collect
  at scale than pairwise comparisons.
- **ORPO** merged the supervised fine-tuning and preference-alignment stages
  into a single training pass, removing the need for a separate SFT warm-start
  before preference tuning.
- **SimPO** removed the reference model dependency entirely, replacing DPO's
  reference-relative reward with a length-normalized reward computed directly
  from the policy, simplifying the pipeline further and reducing memory (no
  frozen reference model copy needed during training).
- Across labs, DPO and its descendants displaced PPO-based RLHF as the
  default alignment technique for most open-weight model releases, precisely
  because of the training stability and engineering simplicity the original
  paper's reparameterization unlocked — PPO-based RLHF remains in use
  primarily at frontier labs with the infrastructure and expertise to run it
  reliably at scale, and is increasingly reserved for cases needing online,
  iterative preference collection.

## How it's actually used in production today

DPO (or one of its simplified descendants — KTO, ORPO, SimPO) is the default
final alignment stage for the overwhelming majority of open-weight
instruction-tuned models released today, applied after standard supervised
fine-tuning on a base model. Its appeal in production is exactly what the
paper promised: it slots into a normal supervised-training pipeline (no RL
infrastructure, no reward model to serve or maintain during training), making
preference alignment achievable by teams without dedicated RL engineering
resources — which is most teams.

## Comparison: PPO-based RLHF vs. DPO

| Dimension | PPO-based RLHF | DPO |
|---|---|---|
| Separate reward model | Required | Not required — reward is implicit in the policy |
| Training loop type | Reinforcement learning (online rollouts) | Supervised, classification-style loss |
| Stability / tuning difficulty | Notoriously sensitive to hyperparameters | Comparable to standard fine-tuning |
| Compute / infrastructure needs | High (policy + reward model + value model, online sampling) | Lower (policy + frozen reference, offline data) |
| Preference data usage | Trains reward model, then used indirectly via RL | Used directly in the training loss |
| Adapts to a policy that has drifted during training | Yes, via online rollouts | No, preference data and reference are fixed upfront |
