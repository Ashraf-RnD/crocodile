## The problem before this paper

By early 2023, the most capable language models — GPT-3, PaLM, Chinchilla — were
closed. You couldn't download their weights, inspect them, or run them on your
own hardware; you could only call them through a paid API. That put a hard
ceiling on what researchers and smaller companies could study or build. There was
also a subtler problem: those huge models (100B+ parameters) were optimized
mainly for training efficiency, not for the cost of actually *using* them
afterward — and a model gets used far more times than it gets trained.

## The core idea, in plain language

Meta's LLaMA team asked a different question than "how do we get the best model
for a fixed training budget?" (the question Chinchilla had just answered). They
asked: "if we know a model will be run millions of times after training, what's
the best model for a fixed *inference* budget?" The answer: train a smaller model
for much longer than the "compute-optimal" point, on far more data than similarly
sized models had ever seen — because a smaller model that's cheaper to run every
single day is worth more in practice than a larger model that's marginally
better but far more expensive to serve.

A useful analogy: imagine two ways to get a great cook. You could hire a
world-class chef who costs a fortune per hour to keep on staff (a huge model,
expensive at inference), or you could take a talented apprentice and have them
practice the same dishes for years until they're excellent — cheaper to keep
around and nearly as good (a smaller model trained on far more data than usual).
LLaMA showed the "apprentice" approach could match or beat much larger models,
and — critically — Meta released the resulting weights for researchers to use.

## Why it mattered

LLaMA's 13B-parameter model reportedly outperformed GPT-3's 175B-parameter model
on most benchmarks, despite being over 10x smaller — a striking demonstration
that training data volume mattered more than raw parameter count once you
account for inference cost. And because the weights were released (initially to
researchers, then more broadly with LLaMA 2), LLaMA became the foundation an
entire ecosystem of open fine-tunes, research, and self-hosted deployments was
built on top of — something a closed API model could never enable.
