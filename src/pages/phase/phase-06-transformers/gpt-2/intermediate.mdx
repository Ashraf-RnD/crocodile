## What actually changed from GPT-1 to GPT-2

The architecture change between GPT-1 and GPT-2 is smaller than most people assume
— the paper's real contribution is scale plus a few specific stabilizing tweaks:

<Mermaid client:visible code={`flowchart LR
  GPT1["GPT-1: 117M params, BookCorpus, post-LN"] --> Changes["Move LayerNorm to input of each sublayer (pre-LN); add final LayerNorm; larger vocabulary; scale up 10x"]
  Changes --> GPT2["GPT-2: up to 1.5B params, WebText, pre-LN"]
  GPT2 --> Result["Strong zero-shot performance across many tasks, no fine-tuning"]`} />

- **Pre-LN instead of post-LN** — moving LayerNorm to the input of each sublayer
  (rather than after the residual addition) improved training stability at the
  larger scales the paper targeted, a change later adopted as the near-universal
  default across the field.
- **An additional LayerNorm after the final self-attention block** — added to keep
  the final residual stream well-scaled before the language modeling head.
- **Modified weight initialization** that accounts for the accumulation effect of
  residual connections across depth, scaling initialization by `1/sqrt(N)` where `N`
  is the number of layers.
- **A larger vocabulary** (~50,257 tokens via byte-level BPE) and **larger context
  window** (1024 tokens, up from 512).
- **Scale**: four model sizes were released, from 117M (same as GPT-1) up to 1.5B
  parameters — at the time, notably large for a publicly discussed language model.

None of these are radical architectural departures — the paper's real experiment is
"take a known-good recipe, scale it up substantially, and evaluate zero-shot
transfer rigorously across many benchmark types," which is a different kind of
contribution than introducing a new mechanism.

## Reproducing the paper's zero-shot evaluation pattern

```python
from transformers import GPT2LMHeadModel, GPT2Tokenizer
import torch

tokenizer = GPT2Tokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2-medium")
model = GPT2LMHeadModel.from_pretrained("gpt2-medium")
model.eval()

# Zero-shot summarization, exactly as evaluated in the paper —
# appending "TL;DR:" as an implicit instruction, without any fine-tuning
article = "Scientists have discovered a new species of frog in the Amazon rainforest..."
prompt = article + "\nTL;DR:"

inputs = tokenizer(prompt, return_tensors="pt")
with torch.no_grad():
    output_ids = model.generate(**inputs, max_new_tokens=30, do_sample=False)
print(tokenizer.decode(output_ids[0][inputs["input_ids"].shape[1]:]))
```

The `"TL;DR:"` trick is directly from the paper — the authors found that appending
this specific string (which appears naturally before human-written summaries all
over the internet) elicits reasonable zero-shot summarization behavior, without
any explicit summarization training or fine-tuning.

## The staged release: a genuinely new kind of decision for the field

Unusually for a research paper at the time, OpenAI did not release the full 1.5B
parameter model immediately, citing concerns about potential misuse for generating
convincing fake text (disinformation, spam, impersonation). Instead, they released
progressively larger checkpoints (117M, then 345M, then 762M) over several months
while studying misuse potential, before eventually releasing the full model. This
staged-release approach was controversial within the research community at the time
— some viewed it as responsible, others as generating hype disproportionate to the
actual model's capability — but it set an early precedent for treating language
model releases as a topic requiring deliberate safety consideration, a practice that
became standard for subsequent frontier model releases across the industry.

## Benchmark results the paper reported

The paper evaluates GPT-2 zero-shot across reading comprehension (CoQA), translation,
summarization (CNN/DailyMail), and question answering, comparing against models
specifically trained/fine-tuned for each task. GPT-2 didn't beat dedicated
fine-tuned state-of-the-art systems on most of these tasks — the point wasn't
"GPT-2 is the best summarizer," it was "a single model trained on nothing but raw
text prediction shows non-trivial zero-shot competence at tasks it was never
explicitly trained on," which was a meaningfully different and more surprising
result than raw benchmark supremacy.
