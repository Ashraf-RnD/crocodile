## The paper's two pretraining objectives

The BERT paper trains a single Transformer encoder on two simultaneous tasks:

<Mermaid client:visible code={`flowchart TB
  Corpus["Unlabeled text corpus (BooksCorpus + English Wikipedia)"] --> MLM["Masked Language Modeling: predict ~15% masked tokens"]
  Corpus --> NSP["Next Sentence Prediction: is sentence B really next?"]
  MLM --> Pretrain["Pretrained BERT checkpoint"]
  NSP --> Pretrain
  Pretrain --> FT1["Fine-tune: classification"]
  Pretrain --> FT2["Fine-tune: NER"]
  Pretrain --> FT3["Fine-tune: question answering"]`} />

1. **Masked Language Modeling (MLM)** — 15% of input tokens are selected; of those,
   80% become `[MASK]`, 10% are replaced with a random token, and 10% are left
   unchanged. The model predicts the original token at every selected position using
   bidirectional context.
2. **Next Sentence Prediction (NSP)** — given sentence pairs, 50% are genuine
   consecutive sentences from the corpus and 50% are a random pairing; the model
   predicts which case it's looking at, using the `[CLS]` token's final
   representation.

## What the paper compared against, and by how much

The paper's headline empirical result was a sweep across the **GLUE benchmark** (a
suite of nine sentence-understanding tasks), **SQuAD** (extractive question
answering), and **SWAG** (commonsense inference) — BERT-large improved GLUE's
average score by several points over the prior best published results at the time,
and pushed SQuAD 1.1 past human-level performance on the paper's leaderboard
metric. Critically, the paper reports these as a **single pretrained checkpoint**
fine-tuned separately per task with only a small task-specific output layer added —
not per-task custom architectures, which was the norm before this paper.

```python
# Reproducing the paper's fine-tuning pattern for GLUE-style classification
from transformers import BertTokenizer, BertForSequenceClassification
import torch

tokenizer = BertTokenizer.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")
model = BertForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased", num_labels=2)

inputs = tokenizer("This movie was fantastic!", return_tensors="pt")
with torch.no_grad():
    logits = model(**inputs).logits
print(torch.softmax(logits, dim=-1))
```

## The paper's ablation study: which pieces actually mattered

A notable strength of the paper is its own ablation section, testing what happens if
you remove NSP, or replace bidirectional MLM pretraining with a left-to-right-only
objective while keeping everything else fixed. The paper's own results already
showed NSP contributed less than MLM to downstream quality — a finding later
confirmed and extended by RoBERTa (which dropped NSP entirely and still improved
results through more data and compute). This is a useful case study in reading
papers critically: even the introducing paper's own ablations sometimes foreshadow
which design choices won't survive follow-up scrutiny.

## Two model sizes, one clear scaling signal

The paper releases both BERT-base (110M parameters, 12 layers) and BERT-large (340M
parameters, 24 layers), and shows BERT-large consistently outperforms BERT-base
across nearly every benchmark — an early, clear data point (predating the later
formalized scaling laws literature) that simply scaling up an already-good
architecture and pretraining recipe reliably improves downstream results.

## Comparison to what came immediately before

| | ELMo (2018) | GPT-1 (2018) | BERT (2018) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Context direction | Shallow bidirectional (concatenated separate LSTMs) | Left-to-right only | Deep, jointly bidirectional |
| Base architecture | BiLSTM | Transformer decoder | Transformer encoder |
| How it's used downstream | Frozen features fed into a task-specific model | Fine-tuned end-to-end | Fine-tuned end-to-end |
| Bidirectionality mechanism | Two separate unidirectional LSTMs, concatenated | None | Masked language modeling |

BERT's core architectural insight over GPT-1 (released the same year) was choosing
an encoder and a masking-based objective specifically to enable *true* joint
bidirectionality, rather than ELMo's shallower approach of just concatenating two
independently-trained directional models.
