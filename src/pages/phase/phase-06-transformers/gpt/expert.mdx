## Scaling laws: the finding that reshaped the field's priorities

Kaplan et al. (2020) showed that GPT-style language model loss follows a remarkably
smooth power-law relationship with model size, dataset size, and compute — largely
independent of many specific architectural choices. This finding reoriented much of
the field's research priorities: rather than searching for clever architectural
tricks, a large fraction of subsequent progress came from scaling known-good
architectures with more data and compute, guided by these power laws to predict
optimal allocation between model size and data before spending the compute.

**Chinchilla** (Hoffmann et al., 2022) refined this further, showing that many
earlier large models (including the original GPT-3) were meaningfully
**undertrained relative to their size** — for a fixed compute budget, a smaller
model trained on more data often outperforms a larger model trained on less data.
This directly changed how subsequent model families (LLaMA, and most open-weight
models since) allocate compute: favoring more tokens per parameter rather than
maximizing parameter count alone.

## Emergent in-context learning: still not fully explained

GPT-3's headline capability — solving new tasks from a handful of examples placed in
the prompt, with no gradient updates — was not explicitly optimized for during
pretraining, which is pure next-token prediction. The mechanistic explanation
remains an active research area, but the leading account (partially supported by
Anthropic's induction-head research) is that certain attention head circuits learned
during pretraining implement a general "complete-the-pattern" computation that,
scaled up, generalizes to genuinely novel in-context tasks. This matters practically:
in-context learning quality is not something you can currently engineer directly —
it's an emergent consequence of scale and data diversity, which is part of why
prompt engineering remains empirical rather than fully principled.

## From base model to assistant: SFT, RLHF, and DPO

A raw pretrained GPT-style model completes text plausibly but has no notion of
"being helpful" or "following instructions" — it will complete a question with more
questions if that's statistically likely, rather than answering it. Turning a base
model into an assistant requires additional stages:

1. **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)** — fine-tune on curated instruction/response
   pairs to bias the model toward an assistant-like response format.
2. **Reward modeling** — train a separate model to score response quality based on
   human preference comparisons.
3. **RLHF (PPO)** — use the reward model to further fine-tune the policy (the
   assistant model) via reinforcement learning, optimizing for human-preferred
   outputs.
4. **DPO (Direct Preference Optimization)** — a more recent, simpler alternative
   that optimizes directly on preference pairs without a separate reward model or
   RL loop, achieving comparable alignment quality with substantially less
   infrastructure complexity.

This pipeline is why "GPT" as a research architecture and "ChatGPT-style assistant"
as a product are distinct things — the architecture is necessary but nowhere near
sufficient for the behavior users experience; alignment training contributes as much
to the end product's usefulness as the base pretraining does.

## Inference cost asymmetry: prefill vs. decode

Serving a GPT-style model splits into two distinct compute regimes: **prefill**
(processing the full prompt in one parallel forward pass — compute-bound, GPU-FLOP
limited) and **decode** (generating each subsequent token one at a time — memory
bandwidth-bound, limited by how fast the KV cache can be read, not by FLOPs
available). This asymmetry is why serving frameworks (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM) treat
prefill and decode as separate scheduling problems, and why techniques like
speculative decoding specifically target the decode phase — prefill is already
efficient by virtue of full parallelism, while decode's sequential nature is the
actual latency bottleneck for long generations.

## Trade-off summary

| Design lever | Effect | Production relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Model size vs. data (Chinchilla-optimal ratio) | Fixed-compute quality ceiling | Guides pretraining budget allocation |
| SFT only vs. SFT + RLHF/DPO | Instruction-following vs. genuinely preference-aligned behavior | Determines assistant quality, safety behavior |
| Greedy vs. sampling decoding | Deterministic/precise vs. diverse/creative output | Task-dependent (code generation vs. creative writing) |
| Full fine-tuning vs. LoRA/QLoRA | Maximum adaptation vs. memory-efficient adaptation | Determines feasible fine-tuning infra/cost |

## The practical engineering takeaway

Most of what makes a modern "GPT-style" product good — helpfulness, safety, format
adherence — comes from post-pretraining alignment stages, not from the base
next-token-prediction architecture itself. Understanding GPT purely as "a decoder
stack trained on next-token prediction" explains its raw language modeling
capability, but explains almost none of what distinguishes a well-aligned production
assistant from a merely fluent, unaligned base model.
