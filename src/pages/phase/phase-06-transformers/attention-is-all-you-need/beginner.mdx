## The paper that started it all

"Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017) introduced the **Transformer** —
the architecture behind essentially every major language model today (BERT, GPT,
T5, and their descendants). Its title is a direct claim: you don't need recurrence
(RNNs/LSTMs) or convolutions to build state-of-the-art sequence models — attention,
used correctly, is enough on its own.

### The problem it solved

Before this paper, the best machine translation systems used RNNs or LSTMs, which
process a sentence one word at a time, in order. This created two real problems:

1. **Slow training** — you can't parallelize across the sequence, since word 5
   depends on having already processed words 1-4. Training on modern GPUs (which
   excel at parallel computation) was underutilized.
2. **Long-range forgetting** — information from early words had to survive many
   sequential steps to influence later predictions, and in practice it often didn't,
   especially for long sentences.

The paper's solution: throw away recurrence entirely and use **self-attention**
(covered in its own lesson) as the core building block. Every word can directly
"look at" every other word in a single step, regardless of distance, and — crucially
— this computation can run in parallel across the whole sequence.

### A simplified before/after picture

```
Before (RNN):  word1 -> word2 -> word3 -> word4 -> word5
               (must process in order, one at a time)

After (Transformer): word1, word2, word3, word4, word5
                      (all processed simultaneously; each one
                       directly attends to every other one)
```

The paper introduced this via an **encoder-decoder** architecture originally built
for machine translation: an encoder reads the source sentence (bidirectionally), and
a decoder generates the translated sentence one word at a time, attending back to
the encoder's output.

## Try it yourself

Based on what you just read, why would translating a very long sentence (say, 50+
words) have been especially hard for an RNN-based translation system, and why does
the Transformer's design directly address that specific weakness?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

In an RNN, information from word 1 has to pass through 49 sequential processing
steps to influence how word 50 is translated — at each step some information is
inevitably lost or distorted, a problem often called "vanishing" long-range
dependencies. In the Transformer, self-attention lets word 50 directly compute a
relationship with word 1 in a single operation, regardless of how many words are in
between — the "path length" between any two words is constant, not proportional to
sentence length. This is precisely why Transformers handle long sequences more
robustly than RNNs did.

</details>
