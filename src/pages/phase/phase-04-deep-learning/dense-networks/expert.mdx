## The universal approximation theorem, and why it doesn't settle the question

The universal approximation theorem states that a single hidden layer with enough
neurons can approximate any continuous function on a bounded domain arbitrarily
well. In practice this result is nearly irrelevant to architecture decisions: it
says nothing about *how many* neurons are needed (which can be exponential), nor
about whether such a network is learnable via gradient descent from a random
initialization. Depth, not just width, matters enormously in practice because deeper
networks can represent the same functions with exponentially fewer total parameters
for functions with compositional structure — which is most of what real-world data
looks like.

## Depth vs. width as an engineering trade-off

| Dimension | Effect of increasing | Practical cost |
|---|---|---|
| Depth (more layers) | Exponentially more expressive for compositional functions | Harder optimization (vanishing/exploding gradients), more sequential latency |
| Width (more neurons/layer) | More capacity per layer, easier optimization | Quadratic parameter growth, more memory, parallelizes well on GPU |

This is why the historical trend (AlexNet -> VGG -> ResNet -> modern transformers)
moved toward deeper networks *combined with* mechanisms (residual connections,
normalization) specifically designed to make depth optimizable — depth alone,
without those mitigations, reliably fails to train past a moderate number of layers.

## Why residual connections changed what "deep" meant

A residual block computes `y = F(x) + x` instead of `y = F(x)`. The `+x` identity
path gives gradients a direct route back to earlier layers that bypasses `F`
entirely, so `dLoss/dx` always has a component of exactly 1 regardless of how poorly
conditioned `F`'s gradient is. This is what allowed networks to scale from ~20 layers
(VGG) to 100+ layers (ResNet) and, later, to the hundreds of transformer blocks in
modern LLMs — dense networks without this mechanism empirically degrade in accuracy
past a certain depth, not just in training difficulty.

## Initialization theory: why Kaiming/Xavier aren't arbitrary

Random initialization must keep the variance of activations (forward) and gradients
(backward) roughly constant across layers — otherwise repeated multiplication by
weight matrices with the wrong scale causes activations to explode or vanish purely
from initialization, before any training has happened. **Xavier/Glorot
initialization** balances variance for symmetric activations (tanh); **Kaiming/He
initialization** accounts for ReLU zeroing out roughly half of activations, scaling
weights by `sqrt(2/fan_in)` to compensate. Getting this wrong is invisible in small
networks (a few layers can absorb the mismatch) but reliably prevents convergence in
deep ones — it's one of the few things that must be correct before training even
starts producing a useful signal.

## Parameter count and FLOPs as first-class design constraints

A dense layer `Linear(in, out)` costs `in * out` parameters and roughly `2 * in * out`
FLOPs per sample. For high-dimensional inputs (raw images, long sequences), this
grows prohibitively — a `Linear(150528, 4096)` layer for a 224x224x3 image already
costs ~600M parameters in one layer alone. This parameter/FLOP cost, not just
theoretical expressiveness, is the direct engineering reason CNNs (parameter
sharing via convolution) and transformers (attention with structured weight reuse)
replaced pure dense architectures for structured, high-dimensional data — dense
layers remain the default only where inputs are already low-dimensional or where
they're used as the final "head" on top of a feature extractor.

## Production concerns beyond accuracy

- **Inference latency**: dense layers are pure matrix multiplies — extremely
  GPU/TPU-friendly, but memory-bandwidth-bound rather than compute-bound at small
  batch sizes, which matters for real-time serving.
- **Quantization robustness**: dense layers generally quantize well (int8) since
  their operation is a simple matmul, making them a common target for aggressive
  compression in edge deployment.
- **Overfitting risk scales with width**: wide dense layers on small datasets
  overfit fast; dropout, weight decay, and reduced width are the standard levers
  before reaching for architectural changes.
