## Receptive field: what a deep neuron actually "sees"

Each convolutional layer only looks at a local neighborhood, but stacking layers
grows the **receptive field** — the region of the original input that influences a
given output neuron — geometrically with depth. For stacked 3x3 convolutions, the
receptive field grows roughly linearly with the number of layers, which is why
architectures prefer several stacked small kernels (3x3) over one large kernel
(7x7): two 3x3 convolutions give the same receptive field as one 5x5 with fewer
parameters (`2 * 9 = 18` vs `25` per channel pair) and an extra non-linearity in
between — strictly more expressive per parameter spent.

## Parameter sharing: why convolution beats dense layers on images

A dense layer connecting a 224x224x3 image to even a modest hidden layer costs
hundreds of millions of parameters. A convolutional layer with the same receptive
field reuses the *same* small filter (say, 3x3x3=27 weights) at every spatial
position — the assumption being that a useful pattern (an edge) is useful wherever
it appears in the image, not just in one fixed location. This inductive bias
(translation equivariance) is precisely what makes CNNs both parameter-efficient and
generalize well on image data without needing to see every possible object position
during training.

## Convolution variants and their trade-offs

| Variant | Mechanism | Parameter cost | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard conv | Full-channel filter at every position | `k^2 * C_in * C_out` | Default, maximum expressiveness |
| Depthwise separable | Depthwise conv (spatial) + 1x1 pointwise conv (channel mixing) | ~`8-9x` fewer params | MobileNet-class efficient/edge models |
| Dilated (atrous) conv | Filter taps spaced apart, expanding receptive field without more params | Same as standard, larger receptive field | Semantic segmentation, audio (WaveNet) |
| Grouped conv | Channels split into groups, each convolved independently | Fraction of standard conv (`1/groups`) | ResNeXt, efficient architectures |
| Transposed conv | Learnable upsampling | Same formula as standard conv | Decoders, generative models, segmentation |

## BatchNorm placement and its interaction with convolution

BatchNorm after a convolution and before the activation is standard, but it changes
the effective function the convolution needs to learn: because BatchNorm rescales
per-channel outputs, the convolution's bias term becomes redundant (BatchNorm's own
learned shift subsumes it) — which is why `nn.Conv2d(..., bias=False)` immediately
followed by `BatchNorm2d` is common in production architectures, saving parameters
with no loss in expressiveness.

## Quantization and inference-time engineering

CNNs are one of the most quantization-friendly architecture families: convolution's
regular, dense compute pattern maps cleanly onto int8 inference kernels with
well-understood accuracy loss (~1% top-1 for common models with post-training
quantization). This is a major reason CNNs remain the default for edge/mobile vision
despite transformer-based vision models (ViT) matching or exceeding accuracy at
scale — CNN inference tooling (TensorRT, CoreML, TFLite) is more mature, and the
inductive bias toward locality means CNNs often need less data and compute to reach
usable accuracy on constrained hardware.

## Data augmentation as an implicit regularizer

Because convolutional weight sharing already encodes translation invariance, most of
the remaining regularization burden in CNN training is handled by data augmentation
(random crop, flip, color jitter) rather than architectural changes — a distinction
worth knowing when debugging an overfitting CNN: architecture-level fixes
(more BatchNorm, more dropout) are often less effective than simply expanding
augmentation, since the failure mode is frequently insufficient data diversity, not
excess model capacity.
