## Convergence rate and condition number

For convex quadratic objectives, gradient descent's convergence rate depends on the
**condition number** of the Hessian — the ratio of its largest to smallest eigenvalue.
An ill-conditioned loss surface (a long, narrow valley) forces gradient descent to
zigzag inefficiently across the narrow direction while making slow progress along the
shallow one, since a single learning rate can't be simultaneously appropriate for both
curvatures. This is precisely the geometric problem momentum and adaptive per-
parameter methods (Adam) are designed to mitigate — not by changing the loss surface,
but by using update history to effectively adapt the step size per direction.

## The edge of stability phenomenon

Classical optimization theory predicts gradient descent diverges once the learning
rate exceeds `2/L` (where `L` is the largest Hessian eigenvalue, i.e. the sharpest
local curvature). Empirical work on neural network training has found that networks
often train stably at learning rates that *should* cause divergence by this
classical bound — training self-organizes toward an "edge of stability" regime where
the local sharpness itself adapts during training to accommodate the learning rate,
rather than the learning rate being constrained by a fixed, static sharpness. This
overturned some earlier assumptions about why large learning rates work at all for
deep networks and is an active empirical research area, not settled theory.

## Implicit regularization from SGD noise

The specific noise structure of mini-batch SGD — not just "any noise," but noise
correlated with the data distribution and batch size — appears to bias training toward
flatter minima, which correlate empirically with better generalization compared to
sharp minima that full-batch gradient descent is more prone to settling into. This
means batch size isn't a pure throughput knob: smaller batches (noisier gradients) can
sometimes produce better-generalizing models even holding total compute constant,
which is why "just increase batch size for more parallelism" isn't a free lunch in
practice, and large-batch training typically pairs with learning-rate scaling rules
(e.g. linear scaling with warmup) specifically to compensate for this effect.

## Trade-off summary

| Choice | Convergence speed | Generalization / stability |
|---|---|---|
| Full-batch GD | Accurate direction, slow per-epoch throughput | Can settle into sharper minima |
| Small-batch SGD | Noisy but cheap per step | Implicit regularization toward flatter minima |
| Large-batch SGD | High throughput (parallelizable) | Needs LR scaling/warmup to match small-batch generalization |
| Second-order methods (L-BFGS) | Fast convergence near minimum | Impractical memory/compute cost at scale |
