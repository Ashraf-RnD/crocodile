## MAP estimation as Bayes' theorem in disguise

**Maximum a posteriori (MAP)** estimation — finding the parameters that maximize
`P(θ | data) ∝ P(data | θ) · P(θ)` — is Bayes' theorem applied to model fitting itself,
not just classification. Crucially, this reframes common "regularization" techniques
as implicit priors:

- L2 regularization (weight decay) corresponds exactly to assuming a Gaussian prior
  on the weights
- L1 regularization corresponds to a Laplace prior, which is why it induces sparsity
  (the Laplace prior has a sharp peak at zero)

This means the regularization strength hyperparameter (`lambda`) is, statistically
speaking, encoding how strongly you believe weights should be small *before* seeing
any data — a Bayesian reading of a purely "engineering" trick.

## Why exact Bayesian inference is usually intractable

For anything beyond simple conjugate models, computing the true posterior requires
integrating over the entire parameter space — `P(data) = ∫ P(data | θ) P(θ) dθ` — which
has no closed form for most useful models, including neural networks. This is the
motivation for two major approximate-inference families:

- **Variational inference**: approximate the true posterior with a simpler family of
  distributions and optimize to minimize KL divergence to it (this is exactly the
  mechanism behind VAEs — the "variational" in the name)
- **MCMC (Markov Chain Monte Carlo)**: sample from the posterior without ever
  computing it in closed form, at the cost of being computationally expensive and slow
  to converge for high-dimensional parameter spaces like deep networks

## Bayesian deep learning and epistemic uncertainty

A standard neural network gives a point estimate — one set of weights, one prediction,
with no notion of "how sure the model is about its own weights" (as opposed to a
softmax probability, which only captures uncertainty *given* those weights). Bayesian
deep learning methods (e.g. Monte Carlo Dropout, deep ensembles as an approximation)
attempt to recover an implicit posterior over weights, letting you distinguish
**aleatoric uncertainty** (irreducible noise in the data) from **epistemic
uncertainty** (the model hasn't seen enough data to be sure) — a distinction that
matters directly for knowing when a model's confidence should be trusted versus when
it needs more training data or a fallback to human review.

## RLHF's KL-constrained objective as a Bayesian posterior

RLHF's typical objective — maximize reward while staying close (via a KL penalty) to a
reference policy — can be read as computing an approximate posterior over policies:
the reference model acts as a prior, and the reward signal acts as a likelihood term
pulling the policy toward higher-reward behavior, with the KL term literally
controlling how much the "posterior" (fine-tuned policy) is allowed to diverge from
the "prior" (base model). This is one of the deeper reasons a too-large KL penalty
coefficient leaves a model unchanged, while too small a one causes reward hacking and
degenerate outputs — it's the prior-vs-likelihood balance playing out directly.
