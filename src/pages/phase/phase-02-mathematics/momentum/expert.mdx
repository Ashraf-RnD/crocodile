## Momentum's convergence rate on ill-conditioned quadratics

For a convex quadratic with condition number `κ` (ratio of largest to smallest
Hessian eigenvalue), plain gradient descent's convergence rate scales as
`O((κ-1)/(κ+1))` per step, while the heavy-ball momentum method achieves
`O((√κ-1)/(√κ+1))` — replacing a linear dependence on `κ` with a square-root
dependence. For a highly ill-conditioned problem (large `κ`, i.e. a long narrow
valley), this is the formal reason momentum can converge dramatically faster than
plain gradient descent — not just an empirical folk observation, but a provable
result for this idealized setting.

## Momentum as discretized second-order ODE dynamics

The continuous-time limit of the heavy-ball momentum update is a second-order
ordinary differential equation, analogous to a physical particle with mass (inertia)
moving in a potential field with friction — this is precisely why the "ball rolling
downhill" intuition isn't just a metaphor but reflects real underlying dynamics.
Plain gradient descent, by contrast, corresponds to a first-order ODE (pure
overdamped motion with no inertia). This connection to physical dynamical systems is
also the lens through which Nesterov's accelerated gradient method is best
understood: it corresponds to a different, more carefully tuned discretization of
essentially the same continuous dynamics, which is why it achieves a provably better
convergence rate for convex problems without changing the core "accumulate velocity"
idea.

## Interaction between momentum and learning rate schedules

Momentum and learning rate are not independent hyperparameters — the *effective* step
size in the direction of consistent gradient flow scales roughly as
`learning_rate / (1 - momentum)`, not just `learning_rate` alone. This means changing
momentum without adjusting learning rate (or vice versa) can silently change the
effective step size by a large factor — a common, easy-to-miss source of training
instability when hyperparameter search treats them as fully independent axes rather
than accounting for this coupling.

## Momentum in non-convex, stochastic settings — the honest gap

The clean convergence-rate results above are for convex, deterministic (full-batch)
gradient descent. In the non-convex, stochastic setting that actual deep learning
operates in, momentum's benefits are empirically robust and widely reproduced, but
the theoretical guarantees are much weaker (typically convergence to a stationary
point, with rate bounds that don't cleanly show the same dramatic κ-dependent
speedup). Practitioner intuition here is running ahead of full theoretical
justification — worth knowing so you don't over-claim precision the theory doesn't
actually provide for this regime.
