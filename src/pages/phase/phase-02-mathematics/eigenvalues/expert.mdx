## Eigenvalues and the Hessian: loss landscape curvature

At a critical point of a loss function, the eigenvalues of the **Hessian** (matrix of
second derivatives) tell you what kind of point it is: all positive eigenvalues means
a local minimum, all negative means a local maximum, and a mix of signs means a
**saddle point**. In high-dimensional neural network loss landscapes, saddle points
vastly outnumber true local minima — this is one of the theoretical reasons plain
gradient descent can stall (near-zero gradient) without actually being near a good
minimum, and why optimizers with momentum help escape these flat directions.

## Spectral radius and RNN gradient stability

For a recurrent update `h_t = f(W h_{t-1})`, the **spectral radius** (largest
eigenvalue magnitude) of `W` determines long-run behavior: if it's less than 1,
repeated multiplication shrinks the signal toward zero (vanishing gradients); if
greater than 1, it grows unbounded (exploding gradients). This is the precise
mathematical reason vanilla RNNs fail on long sequences, and why LSTM/GRU gating
mechanisms exist — they're explicitly designed to give gradients a path with
effective eigenvalue near 1 (the cell state's additive update).

## Power iteration and why you rarely compute exact eigendecomposition

Exact eigendecomposition costs `O(n^3)` — infeasible for the huge matrices in deep
learning. When only the **largest** eigenvalue/eigenvector is needed (e.g. estimating
Lipschitz constants for spectral normalization in GANs), **power iteration** —
repeatedly multiplying a random vector by the matrix and renormalizing — converges to
the dominant eigenvector in `O(n^2)` per iteration, with convergence rate governed by
the ratio between the top two eigenvalues. This is exactly what `torch.nn.utils.spectral_norm`
uses under the hood.

```python
def power_iteration(A, num_iters=50):
    v = torch.randn(A.shape[1])
    for _ in range(num_iters):
        v = A @ v
        v = v / torch.norm(v)
    eigenvalue = v @ A @ v
    return eigenvalue, v
```

## Trade-off summary

| Method | Cost | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| Full eigendecomposition (`eig`/`eigh`) | O(n³) | Small matrices, need all eigenpairs |
| Power iteration | O(n²) per iter | Only need the dominant eigenvalue/vector |
| Lanczos / Krylov methods | O(n²) per iter, better convergence | Need top-k eigenpairs of large sparse matrices |
| Randomized SVD | O(n²k) | Approximate top-k for very large, dense matrices |
