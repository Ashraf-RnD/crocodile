## A matrix as a transformation

Think of a matrix as an action that stretches, rotates, or squishes space. Most
vectors, when transformed by a matrix, change direction. But for some special vectors,
the matrix only **stretches or shrinks** them — it doesn't rotate them at all. That
stretch factor is the **eigenvalue**, and the special vector is the **eigenvector**.

<Mermaid client:visible code={`flowchart LR
  V["Vector v"] --> M["Multiply by matrix A"]
  M --> R1["Usually: direction changes"]
  M --> R2["Special case: A·v = λ·v — only length changes"]`} />

## The equation

```
A · v = λ · v
```

`A` is the matrix, `v` is the eigenvector, `λ` (lambda) is the eigenvalue — a single
number telling you how much that direction gets stretched (`λ > 1`), shrunk
(`0 < λ < 1`), or flipped (`λ < 0`).

```python
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[2, 0],
              [0, 3]])
eigenvalues, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eig(A)
print(eigenvalues)   # [2. 3.]
```

For this diagonal matrix, the eigenvalues are just the diagonal entries — it stretches
the x-direction by 2 and the y-direction by 3, with no rotation at all.

## Why this matters in AI (a preview)

PCA (dimensionality reduction) finds the directions of most "spread" in your data by
computing eigenvalues of the data's covariance matrix — the largest eigenvalues point
to the directions that matter most. You'll meet this again properly in the ML phase.

## Try it yourself

For `A = [[4, 0], [0, 1]]`, without running code: which direction does this matrix
stretch the most?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

The x-direction (first axis), by a factor of 4 — its eigenvalue (4) is larger than
the y-direction's eigenvalue (1).
</details>
