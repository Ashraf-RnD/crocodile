## Teacher forcing vs exposure bias

Training an autoregressive model uses the chain-rule factorization with **teacher
forcing**: at each step, the model conditions on the *true* previous tokens, not its
own (possibly wrong) predictions. At inference time, there's no ground truth to
condition on — the model conditions on its own generated tokens instead. This
train/inference mismatch is **exposure bias**: errors made early in generation shift
the conditioning context away from anything seen during training, and can compound.
This is one of the standing theoretical justifications for techniques like scheduled
sampling, and part of why sampling strategy (temperature, nucleus sampling) at
inference time meaningfully affects output quality beyond just "more randomness."

## Attention as a learned conditional weighting

Self-attention computes, for each token, a probability distribution (via softmax) over
*which other tokens to condition on and how strongly* — the attention weights are
literally a conditional probability-like weighting used to form a context vector. This
reframes attention not as an exotic new mechanism but as a learned, input-dependent
generalization of the fixed conditioning window used by older n-gram language models.

## Simpson's paradox in ML evaluation

Aggregate conditional statistics can reverse when you look at subgroups. A model can
have higher accuracy than a baseline overall, `P(correct | model)  > P(correct | baseline)`,
while being *worse* on every individual subgroup, if the subgroup sizes are unevenly
distributed between the aggregate populations being compared. This is a real, recurring
failure mode in ML evaluation — a new model can look like a win on an aggregate metric
while quietly regressing on your most important segment. Any conditional-probability-based
metric (accuracy, precision by cohort) should be checked at the subgroup level before
trusting the aggregate number, especially when comparing across different traffic mixes
or A/B test arms with non-random assignment.

## The Markov assumption as a conditioning simplification

`P(w_t | w_1, ..., w_{t-1}) ≈ P(w_t | w_{t-k}, ..., w_{t-1})` — conditioning only on the
last `k` tokens instead of the full history — is the Markov assumption underlying
classical n-gram models. Transformers don't make this assumption explicitly (they can
attend to arbitrarily distant tokens within the context window), but the *context
window itself* is a hard Markov-like truncation: anything outside it is unconditionally
unavailable, which is precisely the motivation behind long-context and retrieval-
augmented approaches — they're different strategies for coping with the same
underlying finite-conditioning-window limitation.
