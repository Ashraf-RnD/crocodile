## "Given that..." changes everything

Conditional probability answers: "given that we already know B happened, what's the
probability of A?" — written `P(A | B)`. New information narrows down the possibilities,
which changes the probability.

**Example**: the probability a random email is spam might be 20%. But the probability
it's spam *given that it contains the word "free"* is much higher — that word is
strong evidence.

## The formula

```
P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)
```

```python
p_spam_and_free = 0.14   # 14% of all emails are spam AND contain "free"
p_free = 0.20             # 20% of all emails contain "free"
p_spam_given_free = p_spam_and_free / p_free
print(p_spam_given_free)  # 0.70 — 70% chance of spam, given it contains "free"
```

## Why this matters for language models

Every time an LLM generates the next word, it's sampling from a conditional
probability: `P(next word | all previous words)`. The entire "autoregressive"
generation process — the core mechanism behind GPT-style models — is nothing but
repeatedly computing and sampling from a conditional probability distribution.

## Try it yourself

10% of patients have a disease. A test is positive for 90% of people who have it, and
also positive for 5% of people who don't. If someone tests positive, is it *certain*
they have the disease?

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

No — this needs Bayes' theorem (next lesson) to combine both pieces of information
properly. Spoiler: because the disease is rare, a surprisingly large share of positive
tests are actually false positives from the healthy 90% of the population.
</details>
