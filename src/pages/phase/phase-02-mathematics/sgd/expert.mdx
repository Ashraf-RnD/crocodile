## The linear scaling rule and its limits

Empirically, when increasing batch size by a factor `k`, scaling the learning rate
linearly by the same factor `k` (with a gradual warmup period) approximately preserves
training dynamics — this "linear scaling rule" is the standard recipe for large-batch
distributed training. It breaks down at very large batch sizes: beyond a certain
point, further increasing batch size and learning rate together stops helping and
starts hurting final accuracy, revealing a batch-size regime where SGD's implicit
noise-driven regularization has been scaled away entirely rather than just sped up.

## Distributed SGD: data parallelism and gradient synchronization

Training across multiple GPUs typically uses **data-parallel SGD**: each device holds
a full model replica, processes a different shard of the batch, computes local
gradients, then synchronizes via an **all-reduce** operation (averaging gradients
across all devices) before every device applies the identical update. This keeps model
replicas in sync without a central parameter server, at the cost of communication
overhead — the all-reduce cost is why gradient compression, overlapping communication
with backward-pass computation, and larger local batch sizes are all common
strategies to keep distributed training compute-bound rather than communication-bound.

## SGD as a stochastic differential equation

In the continuous-time limit, SGD's discrete update rule can be approximated as a
stochastic differential equation (SDE) — a deterministic gradient flow term plus an
injected noise term whose variance depends on `learning_rate / batch_size`. This
framing explains, from first principles, why the *ratio* of learning rate to batch
size (not either value alone) is often the more meaningful quantity to reason about
when tuning: the same ratio produces similar effective noise levels and similar
training dynamics, even at very different absolute batch sizes — a useful mental
shortcut when scaling a known-good small-batch recipe up to larger hardware.

## Non-convexity and the honest theoretical picture

Classical SGD convergence guarantees (convergence to a global minimum at a known rate)
apply to convex objectives. Neural network loss landscapes are highly non-convex, and
strict convergence guarantees for this setting are much weaker — typically only
guaranteeing convergence to a stationary point (near-zero gradient), not a global or
even necessarily a good local minimum. That deep learning works as well as it does in
practice, despite this theoretical gap, remains an active area of research explaining
*why* — overparameterization, benign loss landscape geometry in practice, and the
implicit regularization effects discussed elsewhere in this phase are all part of the
current best explanations, not a fully closed theoretical question.
