## OLS assumptions and what breaks

Ordinary least squares (OLS) is the estimator behind `sklearn.linear_model.LinearRegression`. Its
statistical guarantees — unbiasedness, and being the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE) per the
Gauss-Markov theorem — rest on assumptions that are easy to violate silently in practice:

- **Linearity**: the target is a linear combination of the features (plus noise). Violated by
  curved or threshold relationships; diagnose with residual-vs-fitted plots showing curvature.
- **Independence of errors**: residuals aren't correlated with each other. Violated in time series
  or grouped/panel data (e.g. repeated measurements per customer) — leads to underestimated
  standard errors and overconfident inference, even though point predictions may be fine.
- **Homoscedasticity**: error variance is constant across the range of predictions. Violated when
  residual spread grows with fitted value (a funnel shape). Standard errors and confidence
  intervals become unreliable; point estimates from `.fit()` remain unbiased but inefficient.
- **No perfect multicollinearity** (and ideally low multicollinearity): features shouldn't be
  linear combinations of each other. Near-collinearity doesn't break the fit numerically but makes
  individual coefficients unstable and hard to interpret — the model can still predict well while
  individual coefficient signs and magnitudes become meaningless.
- **No endogeneity**: features shouldn't be correlated with the error term (e.g. omitted-variable
  bias). This is the hardest to detect from data alone and usually requires domain reasoning.

Note what's *not* required: normality of errors is only needed for exact-sample hypothesis
testing and confidence intervals, not for the point estimates themselves (which are consistent
under the Gauss-Markov conditions regardless).

## Normal equation vs. gradient descent

Two ways to solve the least-squares problem:

**Closed-form (normal equation)**: minimize `||Xw - y||^2` by solving `w = (X^T X)^-1 X^T y`
directly. This is exact in one step, but computing a matrix inverse is `O(p^3)` in the number of
features `p`, and `X^T X` can be ill-conditioned when features are collinear.

**scikit-learn's actual approach**: `LinearRegression` does not use the naive normal equation or
gradient descent. It calls `scipy.linalg.lstsq`, which uses SVD (singular value decomposition) of
`X` to solve the least-squares problem directly. SVD avoids explicitly forming `X^T X` (which
squares the condition number and amplifies numerical instability) and handles rank-deficient `X`
gracefully by returning the minimum-norm solution. This is why `LinearRegression` has no
`learning_rate` or `max_iter` hyperparameter — it's not an iterative solver.

**Gradient descent** (batch, stochastic, or mini-batch) is used instead when:
- The dataset is too large to fit `X^T X` or run SVD in memory — `SGDRegressor` in scikit-learn
  fits linear regression incrementally, one batch at a time.
- You need online/streaming updates as new data arrives.
- You're adding regularization terms or loss functions where no closed form exists.

For typical tabular datasets (thousands to low millions of rows, hundreds of features),
`LinearRegression`'s SVD-based solve is both faster and numerically more stable than hand-rolled
gradient descent.

## Regularization trade-offs

Plain OLS has zero bias (asymptotically) but can have high variance when features are numerous,
correlated, or the sample size is small relative to feature count. Regularization intentionally
introduces bias to reduce variance — usually a net win for predictive accuracy out of sample.

## Trade-off summary

| Property | OLS | Ridge (L2) | Lasso (L1) | ElasticNet (L1+L2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bias | None (unbiased) | Increases with alpha | Increases with alpha | Increases with alpha |
| Variance | Highest, especially with collinearity | Reduced, shrinks correlated coefs together | Reduced, but unstable selection under correlation | Reduced, more stable selection than Lasso |
| Sparsity (feature selection) | No | No — shrinks but never zeros coefficients | Yes — drives coefficients to exactly zero | Yes, tunable via `l1_ratio` |
| Handles multicollinearity | Poorly — unstable coefficients | Well — spreads weight across correlated features | Arbitrarily picks one of a correlated group | Best of both — groups correlated features, some sparsity |
| Interpretability | High if assumptions hold | Moderate — all features retained | High — fewer nonzero coefficients | Moderate |
| Solver in sklearn | SVD-based least squares | Closed-form or `sag`/`saga`/`lsqr` | Coordinate descent (`Lasso`) | Coordinate descent (`ElasticNet`) |
| When to reach for it | Small p, low collinearity, need unbiased inference | Many correlated features, want to keep them all | Want automatic feature selection | Correlated features + want sparsity |

`alpha` in Ridge/Lasso/ElasticNet controls the strength of the penalty and should be tuned via
cross-validation (`RidgeCV`, `LassoCV`, `ElasticNetCV`), not picked by hand.

## Production concerns

**Feature drift**: a linear model's coefficients encode the relationships present in training
data at a point in time. If the input distribution shifts (e.g. average house size in a market
changes, or a feature's units change upstream), predictions degrade silently — there's no
mechanism inside `LinearRegression` to detect this. Monitor input feature distributions
(population stability index, KS-test) and prediction distributions in production, not just
offline accuracy at training time.

**Extrapolation risk**: linear regression has no built-in awareness of its training data's
support. Given a feature value far outside the training range, it will happily extrapolate the
line — sometimes producing predictions that are directionally nonsensical (e.g. negative prices).
Guard with explicit range checks on inputs before scoring, or fall back to a documented default
behavior outside the trained range.

**Interpretability vs. black-box trade-off**: a fitted linear model's coefficients are directly
inspectable — "each additional bedroom adds $8,000, holding other features constant" — which is
valuable for debugging, stakeholder trust, and regulatory scrutiny (credit scoring, insurance
pricing, healthcare) where "why did the model say this" has legal weight. Gradient-boosted trees
or neural nets often out-predict linear regression on raw accuracy, but require SHAP/LIME-style
post-hoc explanation layers to approach the same level of transparency, and even those are
approximations rather than exact decompositions.

**When linear regression is the right production choice**:
- As a baseline to beat before justifying the complexity of a more expensive model.
- In regulated domains where model decisions must be explained to auditors or customers by
  contract or law.
- When the relationship genuinely is close to linear and the marginal accuracy gain from a
  complex model doesn't justify its latency, training cost, or maintenance burden.
- When you need fast, cheap inference at scale — a linear model is a dot product; there's no
  faster general-purpose predictor.
- When you need well-calibrated, statistically grounded uncertainty estimates (confidence and
  prediction intervals), which are mathematically well-defined for OLS under its assumptions in a
  way that's much harder to obtain rigorously from tree ensembles or deep models.
