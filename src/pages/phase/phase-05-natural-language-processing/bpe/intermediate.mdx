## The training algorithm, precisely

BPE training is a greedy, iterative merge process over a word-frequency table built
from a training corpus:

<Mermaid client:visible code={`flowchart TB
  A["Start: every word split into characters"] --> B["Count all adjacent symbol pairs across the corpus"]
  B --> C["Find the single most frequent pair"]
  C --> D["Add that pair as a new merge rule + vocabulary entry"]
  D --> E["Replace all occurrences of the pair with the merged symbol"]
  E --> F{"Reached target\nvocab size?"}
  F -->|No| B
  F -->|Yes| G["Final vocabulary + ordered merge rules"]`} />

The output isn't just a vocabulary — it's an **ordered list of merge rules**. At
inference time, tokenizing a new word means applying those merges in the exact order
they were learned, which is why BPE tokenization is deterministic and fast (no search,
just rule application).

## Training your own BPE tokenizer

```python
from tokenizers import Tokenizer, models, trainers, pre_tokenizers

tokenizer = Tokenizer(models.BPE())
tokenizer.pre_tokenizer = pre_tokenizers.Whitespace()

trainer = trainers.BpeTrainer(
    vocab_size=10000,
    min_frequency=2,
    special_tokens=["[UNK]", "[CLS]", "[SEP]", "[PAD]", "[MASK]"],
)

tokenizer.train(files=["corpus.txt"], trainer=trainer)
tokenizer.save("my-bpe-tokenizer.json")

output = tokenizer.encode("Byte-pair encoding is efficient")
print(output.tokens)
```

`vocab_size` is the single most important hyperparameter here — it directly
determines how many merges are learned, and therefore how coarse or fine-grained the
final tokenization is.

## Byte-level BPE: the trick GPT-2 introduced

Character-level BPE still needs a fallback for characters outside its training
alphabet (emoji, rare Unicode scripts) — usually `[UNK]`. GPT-2 solved this
permanently by operating on **raw bytes** (256 possible values) instead of Unicode
characters as the base alphabet:

```python
from transformers import AutoTokenizer

tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2")
# Any byte sequence is representable -- no [UNK] token exists in GPT-2's vocab
print(tokenizer.tokenize("héllo 🌍"))
# ['h', 'é', 'llo', 'ĠðŁ', 'Į', 'į']  -- unusual-looking but fully reversible
print(tokenizer.decode(tokenizer.encode("héllo 🌍")))
# "héllo 🌍" -- exact round-trip, even for the emoji
```

Because every possible input byte sequence is representable, byte-level BPE
guarantees the tokenizer can encode *any* text and decode it back exactly — a
property plain character-level BPE cannot offer without a lossy `[UNK]` fallback.

## Vocabulary size as a tuning knob, not a fixed constant

Unlike WordPiece or Unigram, BPE's stopping criterion is purely "stop when the
vocabulary reaches `vocab_size`" — there's no separate likelihood objective being
optimized. In practice, teams tune `vocab_size` against real trade-offs:

- **Smaller vocab** (e.g. 8k-16k): shorter merge list, faster to train, but longer
  token sequences at inference (more sub-word splits) — increases compute per input
  in transformer attention layers
- **Larger vocab** (e.g. 50k-100k+): shorter sequences per input, but a bigger
  embedding table and output projection matrix, and increased risk of overfitting the
  vocabulary to the training corpus's specific domain

This is why domain-specific tokenizers (e.g. for biomedical or code corpora) are
often trained from scratch with a vocabulary tuned to that domain's vocabulary
distribution, rather than reusing a general-purpose BPE tokenizer like GPT-2's.
