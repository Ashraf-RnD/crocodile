## Turning a whole sentence into one meaningful vector

Word2Vec gives you a vector per *word*. But often you need to compare whole
*sentences* — "how similar are these two questions?" A **sentence embedding**
compresses an entire sentence into a single fixed-size vector, chosen so that
sentences with similar meaning end up with similar vectors, no matter how they're
worded.

Think of it like a movie's one-line summary: two movies with completely different
dialogue can still have nearly identical loglines if their plots are similar. Sentence
embeddings are the model's way of writing that "logline vector" for any piece of text.

### Comparing sentences with cosine similarity

```python
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util

model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")

sentences = [
    "The cat sat on the mat.",
    "A kitten was resting on the rug.",
    "The stock market crashed today.",
]

embeddings = model.encode(sentences)
print(embeddings.shape)
# (3, 384) -- 3 sentences, each a 384-number vector

similarity = util.cos_sim(embeddings[0], embeddings[1])
print(similarity)
# tensor([[0.72]])  -- high: similar meaning, totally different words

similarity2 = util.cos_sim(embeddings[0], embeddings[2])
print(similarity2)
# tensor([[0.05]])  -- low: unrelated topics
```

Notice sentence 1 and 2 share almost no words in common (`"cat"` vs `"kitten"`,
`"mat"` vs `"rug"`), yet the model correctly scores them as highly similar — that's
the whole point of a *semantic* embedding rather than a keyword-matching approach.

### Where this shows up in real products

Sentence embeddings power semantic search ("find documents about X" even when they
don't contain the word X), duplicate question detection, and recommendation systems
that group similar items by meaning rather than exact text match.

## Try it yourself

Using `SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")`, embed the sentences
`"How do I reset my password?"` and `"I forgot my login credentials"`, then compute
their cosine similarity. Predict whether it will be high or low before running it.

<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>

```python
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util

model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
emb = model.encode([
    "How do I reset my password?",
    "I forgot my login credentials",
])
print(util.cos_sim(emb[0], emb[1]))
# tensor([[0.6x]]) -- fairly high. The two sentences share no exact
# words but describe the same underlying problem, which is exactly
# what a well-trained sentence embedding model is designed to capture.
```
