## Why you can't just average BERT's token embeddings

A naive approach to sentence embeddings: run BERT, average the token embeddings from
its last layer. This works poorly in practice — raw BERT embeddings are optimized for
masked-token prediction, not sentence-level similarity, and the resulting vectors are
famously **anisotropic** (clustered in a narrow cone), meaning almost any two
sentences score as suspiciously similar under cosine similarity. Sentence-BERT (SBERT)
fixed this by fine-tuning BERT specifically for the similarity task using a **siamese
network** structure:

<Mermaid client:visible code={`flowchart TB
  S1[Sentence A] --> B1[BERT encoder]
  S2[Sentence B] --> B2["BERT encoder (shared weights)"]
  B1 --> P1[Mean pooling]
  B2 --> P2[Mean pooling]
  P1 --> U[Embedding A]
  P2 --> V[Embedding B]
  U --> Loss["Loss: pull similar pairs together,\npush dissimilar pairs apart"]
  V --> Loss`} />

Both sentences pass through the *same* encoder (shared weights, hence "siamese"),
producing two embeddings that are compared with a similarity-aware loss (contrastive
loss, cosine similarity loss, or triplet loss depending on the training data
available). This is a fundamentally different training objective from BERT's original
masked-language-modeling pretraining, which is why fine-tuned SBERT-style models
outperform raw BERT for retrieval and similarity tasks by a wide margin.

## Using the library in practice

```python
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util
import numpy as np

model = SentenceTransformer("all-mpnet-base-v2")  # stronger, slower than MiniLM

corpus = [
    "How do I reset my password?",
    "What is the refund policy?",
    "How can I change my shipping address?",
    "I forgot my login credentials.",
]
corpus_embeddings = model.encode(corpus, convert_to_tensor=True)

query = "I can't remember my account password"
query_embedding = model.encode(query, convert_to_tensor=True)

hits = util.semantic_search(query_embedding, corpus_embeddings, top_k=2)
for hit in hits[0]:
    print(corpus[hit["corpus_id"]], hit["score"])
# "How do I reset my password?" 0.71
# "I forgot my login credentials." 0.65
```

`util.semantic_search` does a brute-force cosine similarity search — fine for
thousands of documents, but for real production-scale retrieval (millions of
documents), embeddings are typically indexed with an approximate nearest-neighbor
library (FAISS, HNSW, Annoy, or a managed vector database) instead of comparing
against every candidate at query time.

## Model size vs. quality trade-off

Sentence-Transformers ships models spanning a wide speed/accuracy range:

- `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` — 6 layers, ~80MB, very fast, good enough for most semantic
  search and deduplication tasks
- `all-mpnet-base-v2` — 12 layers, larger, meaningfully better embedding quality at
  roughly 3-5x the inference cost
- Domain/task-specific fine-tunes (e.g. `multi-qa-mpnet-base-dot-v1` for QA
  retrieval) — trained on retrieval-specific data, often outperforming general-purpose
  models on that exact task

Choosing a model is a latency/cost/quality trade-off exactly like choosing between a
small and large LLM — bigger isn't automatically better if the smaller model already
clears your task's accuracy bar at a fraction of the compute.

## Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is built on this

This is the technology underneath most RAG pipelines: documents are embedded once and
stored in a vector index; a user's query is embedded at request time; the top-k most
similar document chunks are retrieved and passed to an LLM as context. Sentence
embedding quality directly bounds RAG quality — no amount of prompt engineering on the
generation side can recover from the wrong documents being retrieved in the first
place.
