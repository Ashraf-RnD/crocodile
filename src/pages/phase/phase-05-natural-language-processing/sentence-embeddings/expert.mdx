## The training objective determines everything downstream

A sentence embedding model's usefulness is entirely a function of what it was trained
to optimize for — cosine similarity between embeddings is only meaningful if the
model's loss function explicitly shaped the embedding space around that metric.
**SimCSE** demonstrated a striking result: a purely *unsupervised* contrastive
objective — passing the same sentence through the encoder twice with different
dropout masks (two independently "noised" forward passes act as a free positive
pair) and pushing those two views together while pushing other in-batch sentences
apart — produces embeddings competitive with fully supervised approaches on semantic
textual similarity benchmarks. This reframes what a sentence embedding "is": not a
byproduct of language modeling, but the direct result of shaping a metric space
around a specific notion of similarity — and different notions of similarity
(paraphrase, topical relatedness, entailment, retrieval relevance) genuinely produce
different embedding spaces, even from the same base encoder.

## Symmetric vs. asymmetric search: a frequent production bug

A subtle but critical distinction: **symmetric search** (query and corpus are the same
kind of text, e.g. "find a similar question") versus **asymmetric search** (query and
corpus differ structurally, e.g. a short question retrieving a long passage that
answers it). Using a model trained for symmetric similarity in an asymmetric retrieval
setting (or vice versa) silently degrades retrieval quality — the embedding space was
never shaped to bring short queries and long answer passages close together. This is
why dedicated QA/retrieval models (e.g. `multi-qa-mpnet-base-dot-v1`, or dual-encoder
models trained specifically on query-passage pairs like DPR) exist as a distinct model
family from general sentence-similarity models — and it's a common, hard-to-diagnose
root cause of "our RAG retrieval quality is bad" when the wrong embedding model family
was selected for the access pattern.

## Pooling strategy is not a minor implementation detail

Converting a sequence of token embeddings into one sentence vector requires a pooling
choice, and it materially changes results:

| Pooling strategy | Behavior | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| `[CLS]` token | Uses the first token's final-layer representation | Only meaningful if the model was specifically trained with a `[CLS]`-based objective (e.g. next-sentence prediction); often poor for embedding tasks otherwise |
| Mean pooling | Averages all token embeddings (masking out padding) | Standard default in Sentence-Transformers; robust across sentence lengths |
| Max pooling | Takes the max activation per dimension across tokens | Captures salient features but is more sensitive to outlier tokens/noise |

Sentence-Transformers defaults to mean pooling specifically because SBERT's original
ablations showed it outperforming `[CLS]`-pooling for models not explicitly
pretrained with a `[CLS]`-centric objective — but this is a model-family-specific
empirical finding, not a universal law, and needs re-validating for any new base
encoder.

## Dimensionality, quantization, and vector database economics

Embedding dimensionality (384 for MiniLM, 768-1536+ for larger models) directly
drives vector database storage and search cost at scale — for a corpus of 100M
documents, the difference between 384-dim and 1536-dim float32 vectors is roughly
150GB vs. 600GB of raw storage, before indexing overhead. This has driven active
production engineering work: **Matryoshka Representation Learning** (used in newer
OpenAI and some open embedding models) trains embeddings so that truncating the vector
to a shorter prefix still yields a usable, if slightly lower-quality, embedding —
letting a single model serve both a cheap coarse-ranking pass (truncated vector) and
an expensive precise pass (full vector), instead of needing two separately trained
models. Product quantization and scalar quantization (compressing float32 components
to int8 or binary) are the other major lever, trading a small, measurable recall drop
for 4-32x storage and search-speed improvements — a decision every large-scale
semantic search system has to make explicitly rather than defaulting to full-precision
vectors.

## Evaluating embedding models isn't a single number

MTEB (Massive Text Embedding Benchmark) evaluates embedding models across dozens of
tasks (classification, clustering, retrieval, STS, reranking) because a model that
tops the leaderboard on semantic textual similarity can rank near the bottom on
retrieval, and vice versa — the aggregate MTEB score is a summary that can hide
task-specific weaknesses relevant to your actual use case. The expert practice is to
evaluate candidate embedding models directly on a held-out sample of *your* retrieval
task (with your actual document lengths, domain vocabulary, and query patterns)
rather than trusting a general leaderboard ranking to transfer — embedding models are
notoriously domain-sensitive, and a model trained mostly on web/Wikipedia text can
underperform meaningfully on legal, medical, or code-heavy corpora without any
warning sign beyond a direct evaluation.
