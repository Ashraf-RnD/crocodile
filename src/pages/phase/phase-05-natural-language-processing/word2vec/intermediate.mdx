## Two training objectives: CBOW vs. Skip-gram

Word2Vec isn't one algorithm, it's a training setup with two variants that predict in
opposite directions using a shallow (one hidden layer) neural network:

- **CBOW** (Continuous Bag of Words) predicts the center word from its surrounding
  context — faster to train, works better on frequent words.
- **Skip-gram** predicts surrounding context words from the center word — slower, but
  noticeably better for rare words, since each rare-word occurrence generates several
  training examples (one per context word) instead of just one.

Skip-gram is the more commonly used variant in practice because rare-word quality
usually matters more than raw training speed.

## Negative sampling: the trick that makes it fast

Naively, each training step would require a softmax over the entire vocabulary
(hundreds of thousands of words) — far too slow. **Negative sampling** reframes
training as a much cheaper binary classification: for each true (word, context) pair,
sample a handful of random "negative" words and train the model to distinguish real
context words from noise.

```python
from gensim.models import Word2Vec

sentences = [
    ["the", "quick", "brown", "fox", "jumps", "over", "the", "lazy", "dog"],
    ["the", "dog", "barks", "at", "the", "fox"],
    # ... thousands more tokenized sentences
]

model = Word2Vec(
    sentences,
    vector_size=100,     # embedding dimensionality
    window=5,             # context window size
    min_count=2,          # ignore words appearing fewer than 2 times
    sg=1,                 # 1 = skip-gram, 0 = CBOW
    negative=10,           # negative samples per positive example
    epochs=10,
    workers=4,
)

model.save("my_word2vec.model")
print(model.wv.most_similar("fox", topn=5))
```

Negative sampling with 5-20 samples per step is roughly as effective as full softmax
for downstream quality, at a small fraction of the compute — this is the single
optimization that made training word embeddings on billion-word corpora practical.

## Why Word2Vec embeddings became a standard building block

Before pretrained language models existed, training your own Word2Vec vectors (or
downloading GloVe/Word2Vec pretrained ones) on a large general corpus, then using them
as the frozen input layer of a downstream classifier (LSTM, CNN, or even logistic
regression over averaged vectors) was the default way to get any semantic signal into
a model without needing labeled data for every task. This "pretrain once, reuse
everywhere" pattern is the direct conceptual ancestor of today's pretrained
transformer + fine-tuning workflow — Word2Vec was where the industry first learned
that unsupervised pretraining on raw text transfers well to downstream supervised
tasks.

## A known limitation: one vector per word

Word2Vec gives `"bank"` exactly one vector, blending "river bank" and "financial bank"
into a single averaged representation — it has no way to disambiguate meaning by
context. This static, context-free nature is precisely the gap that contextual
embeddings from transformer models (covered in the sentence-embeddings and Hugging
Face Transformers topics) were built to close.
